## Abstract

Smedberg, C-A. 2024. *Wolves of the Sea: Kóryos, Rock Art, and Maritime Warrior Ideology in the Nordic Bronze Age.*

This thesis aims to situate kóryos, a hypothesized Indo-European (IE) warband of elite male youths, in the Nordic Bronze Age (NBA). This is achieved by constructing a kóryos ideal type using several varied sources, such as historical accounts, comparative linguistic studies, and the mythology of IE-speaking groups, and comparing it with common NBA rock art motifs. Using a theoretical framework of ideology and magic, the results of the comparative analysis are used to hypothesize the praxis, rituals, and ideology of NBA-specific kóryos groups. Based on stylistic and structural similarities, it is argued that kóryos groups and members were part of the elite in NBA society. NBA-specific kóryos members may have engaged in weapon dancing, nudity, and states of ecstatic fury in ritual settings prior to maritime-based voyages and raids. Kóryos may have functioned as a school for the elite where members engaged in raiding and the promulgation of kóryos ideology through rituals and rock art.

**Keywords:** kóryos, Nordic Bronze Age, rock art, ideology, nudity, weapon dancing, elite

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1. Introduction

In recent years, genetic research and aDNA studies have expanded the archaeological field in several ways, especially within the field of Indo-European (IE) studies. A landmark aDNA study by Haak et al. (2015) shows that a massive influx of steppe ancestry from southern Russia and eastern Ukraine spread throughout Europe around 3000 BCE, largely replacing the previous genetic ancestry present in the European Neolithic and becoming fully established in the Bronze Age (BA). Several genetic, archaeological, and linguistic studies support the conclusion that this migration of steppe ancestry from the Pontic-Caspian steppe served as the main vector for the spread of IE languages and cultures in Eurasia (Allentoft et al. 2015, Kristiansen et al. 2017, Cassidy et al. 2016, Anthony 2007, Mallory 1989). Although the details of this migration and cultural spread are debated (Heyd 2017, Furholt 2019), the findings justify an IE perspective on the Nordic Bronze Age (NBA, 1700-500 BCE).

Independently from aDNA studies, the older field of comparative Indo-European linguistics and mythology has provided an insight into the culture of Proto-Indo-European (PIE) peoples. This is based on the reasoning that if a cultural trait can be attested in several historic IE groups, it is most likely descended from a common PIE ancestor, thus tracing it back 5000 years to pre-migration PIE-speaking groups (Anthony 2007: 14ff). One of these is the hypothesized institutional warband of young, wolflike men, known as \*kóryos.

Kóryos is a reconstructed PIE word translated as "army, warband, unit of warriors" (Mallory 2006: 91). The term denotes a practice in several IE cultures in which groups of adolescent boys and teenagers from elite families were initiated into warbands by ages ten and 15 to 20 years old. These bands were seasonally sent to live on the margins of society as outlaws, and members were allowed to raid, destroy, and steal from other societies. Through initiation rites during the winter solstice, the youths would ritually die and symbolically shapeshift into wolves or dogs, thus becoming liminal symbols of death and war. Following initiation, the lives of these adolescents would be centered around warfare, raiding, ritual, magic, and the reciting of heroic poetry and myth. After a period of a few years, the young men would cast off their wolflike identities and reintegrate into society as warriors and leaders (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 42-51, Kershaw 2000, West 2007: 448, Cebrián 2010). While the word kóryos has been reconstructed by modern linguists, the concept behind it has been attested in several IE languages, such as the *koiranoi* in Greek, *fían* in Celtic, *vrātyas* in Sanskrit, *berserkir* and *úlfheðnar* in Old Norse, and *iuvenes* or *Salii* in Italic (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 43, Cebrián 2010: 344, West 2007: 449, Schjødt 2020: 561).

Kóryos is largely unexplored in archaeological studies, barring a few examples (Anthony & Brown 2017, Kaliff & Østigård 2022a). This is partly due to the field's troubled research history, but also because many characteristics associated with kóryos-like groups, such as nudity, weapon dancing, and states of ecstatic fury, are invisible in archaeological sources. However, rock art may offer a method of exploring kóryos in the NBA. Warriors depicted in NBA rock art share several similarities with kóryos attributes of later periods, especially from Germanic and Celtic contexts, but with a maritime focus. This may indicate that maritime kóryos groups were central in the socioeconomic structure of the NBA as warriors, raiding parties, and future leaders.

### 1.2. Research Aims and Questions

In recent years, there has been a growing body of literature surrounding the socioeconomic structures of NBA society, specifically focusing on the interconnected systems of trade, long-distance travel, and warfare. Warriors are argued to have served a central role in these structures, and rock art has been especially utilized in much of the research (Ling et al. 2018a, 2018b, 2022, Horn & Kristiansen 2018, Kristiansen & Earle 2022). Furthermore, new genetic evidence has contributed to the growing field of archaeolinguistics, in which IE linguistics and myths are used to interpret archaeological material and contexts, especially in NBA and BA research (Kristiansen & Kroonen 2023). In contrast, linguists and historians of religions have explored the IE concept of kóryos warbands for more than a century, situating the practice in many IE contexts and arguing for its existence in PIE- and post-PIE-speaking groups (Schroeder 1908, McCone 1987, Kershaw 2000). Despite this, kóryos has largely been unexplored in BA research, barring a few examples (Anthony & Brown 2017, Kaliff & Østigård 2022a, Kristiansen 2022). Furthermore, no extensive study of kóryos and NBA rock art has been made, despite the latter's important role in NBA research. The purpose of this thesis is to contribute to the burgeoning archaeolinguistic field in NBA research with new perspectives and analyses by exploring kóryos in the NBA.

In this thesis, I aim to situate kóryos groups in the NBA. Specifically, I will compare and identify similarities between kóryos attributes and rock art motifs from the NBA, which will serve as my main approach. As Ling (2008: 185ff) argues, many researchers draw conclusions about NBA society based solely on rock art motifs. Because of this, I will also present a structural analysis of warriorship in the NBA to avoid overdetermining rock art's role in my interpretations. The results of the comparative analysis will be used to situate kóryos in the NBA and to explore the praxis, rituals, and ideology of NBA-specific kóryos groups. I will answer the following questions:

> From an Indo-European comparative perspective, what are the most common attributes of kóryos groups? What are the similarities between these kóryos attributes and rock art motifs from the Nordic Bronze Age? What can these similarities inform about the praxis, rituals, and ideology of hypothesized kóryos groups in the NBA?

### 1.3. Research Background

#### 1.3.1. Kóryos and Indo-European Studies

In this section, I will present a brief overview of the research history of kóryos. With a firm rooting in the IE field, the topic has largely only been explored by linguists and historians of religion specializing in comparative IE studies. It is only in recent years that the concept has been more actively explored in archaeology, which stems from the recent surge of aDNA studies linking the proliferation of steppe ancestry throughout Eurasia in the 3rd and 2nd millennium BCE with IE languages (Allentoft et al. 2015, Cassidy et al. 2016).

IE studies trace their origins back to the orientalist Sir William Jones who, in 1786, highlighted the similarities between languages such as Sanskrit, Latin, Greek, Gothic, and Celtic, theorizing that they might share a common source (Momma 1999: 162ff). In 1813, the term "Indo-European" was coined to describe this source. It became particularly popular in linguistic studies (Arvidsson 2000: 33). IE studies became its own established field by the mid-19th century, with German linguist August Schleicher's systemic comparative study of several IE daughter languages, culminating in a reconstructed proto-language (Kaliff & Østigård 2020: 103). IE studies since then follow a general methodology:

> [If a word for a certain cultural feature, object or creature is attested with regular sound correspondences throughout the Indo-European family, we may conclude that the corresponding concept existed before the dispersal of the proto-language. Thus, language is a source of insight into prehistoric cultures and ancient populations, their living conditions, social structure, material culture and religious beliefs (Olsen et al. 2019: 2–3).

Based on this, linguists have, since the 19th century, used the comparative method to reconstruct socioeconomic and cultural elements that share a common IE root. One of these cultural elements is the concept of kóryos – or the more infamous German synonym, *Männerbund* (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 9). The term *Männerbund* was coined in 1902 by Heinrich Schurtz to describe secret male organizations in "primitive" societies (Harris 2008: 290). However, the term quickly gained use within IE research. Scholars such as Schroeder (1908), Weiser (1927), Höfler (1934), and Wikander (1938) argued for the existence of specifically IE *Männerbund* groups in Germanic, Celtic, and Indo-Iranian contexts (Lincoln 2008: 196, Arvidsson 2000: 223–30).

However, the field of IE studies has been fraught with ideological misuse. The term "Aryan" was popularized as a synonym for "Indo-European" during the 19th century. Aryan was a particularly attractive term to some scholars, as it described a noble and superior people rather than a geographic area. During this time, the field started to become co-opted by racial anthropologists using the research to justify racist prejudices - a practice that continued up until the middle of the 20th century (Arvidsson 2000: 34ff, 55). Research on kóryos did not escape this appropriation (Arvidsson 2000: 231). Several researchers, such as Höfler, would propagate a glorified version of kóryos within the SS Ahnenerbe, Nazi Germany's prehistoric research unit, as a basis for racial theories (Lincoln 2008: 196, 1998: 187ff). The influence of kóryos research on Nazi identity was manifested in several militaristic contexts. For instance, the Hitler Youth and the paramilitary organization SA partially modeled themselves on ethnographic studies of the German *Männerbund*, and Hitler would often refer to himself as "Herr Wolf" (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 220ff).

The ideological misuse of kóryos research should not make it unusable in modern research. The field has been reconstituted today and much of the research on kóryos made before 1945 has been revised and saved from the ideological taint of Nazism by modern researchers (Heizmann 2002: 130). Kim McCone (1986, 1987, 2002, 2012), Bruce Lincoln (1981, 1991), and Kris Kershaw (2000), to name a few, have convincingly shown that kóryos groups did exist in several IE contexts based on textual sources. This reflects an institution that most likely traces its origins to PIE groups of the Pontic-Caspian steppe. It is their research and work with a similar approach that will be utilized in this thesis.

#### 1.3.2. Kóryos in Archaeology

As mentioned in the previous section, the term kóryos has only in recent years been extensively explored in archaeological studies. The purpose of this section is to present a research history of studies interpreting archaeological material and contexts with the theoretical framework of kóryos. Although it is a short history, there has been an increase in studies exploring kóryos in the archaeological record due to the previously mentioned aDNA advancements. This is a development that will most likely continue as the interdisciplinary field of IE archaeolinguistics continues to reshape how archaeologists approach the past (Kroonen & Kristiansen 2023: 329).

The kóryos "package", meaning age-sets, wolf-like identities, connections to death, martial prowess, and lawlessness, has only recently been explored in archaeological studies. Historians of religion and linguists have used some archaeological material, such as the 1st century BCE Gundestrup cauldron, to support their arguments based mainly on textual sources (McCone 2012: 28ff). The opposite is rarer. Most often, archaeologists will invoke a more general connection between warriors, warbands, IE migrations, and social institutions (Anthony 2007: 18), or employ it alongside non-IE warbands as a comparative analogy (Vandkilde 2006: 396–403). For the former, one of the earliest archaeologists to make this interpretation is Marija Gimbutas. Already in 1956, Gimbutas connected the spread of IE languages with a mass movement of people from the Pontic-Caspian steppe in the Neolithic, a culture she dubbed the "Kurgan Culture" (Gimbutas 1956: 12ff, 163). She argued that the Kurgans were patriarchal and warlike. In contrast, pre-IE societies, or "Old Europe", were matriarchal and peaceful. Although she never mentioned kóryos by name, she specifically connected cultural change and the spread of IE languages in the Neolithic with mobile IE warbands (Gimbutas 1997: 351-72). Although her dichotomous characterization of peaceful Old Europeans and violent Indo-Europeans has been questioned, later aDNA studies and archaeological finds have only strengthened her theory regarding the Indo-Europeanization of large parts of Eurasia (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 81).

A recent archaeologist exploring the kóryos package is David W. Anthony. Like Gimbutas, Anthony (2007) argued for a Pontic-Caspian steppe homeland for the original PIE speakers. He interprets the Late Bronze Age site of Krasnosamarskoe in the Samara region of Russia as an initiation ritual of a kóryos group. Briefly touched upon in his earlier work (Anthony 2007: 410ff), and later expounded upon in later articles (Anthony & Brown 2017, Brown & Anthony 2019), the site of Krasnosamarskoe is one of the strongest archaeological pieces of evidence for kóryos. The site, dated to 1900-1700 BCE, contains at least 51 dogs and seven wolves that were butchered and eaten close to, or during, the winter solstice. Most of the dogs were mature males who showed no signs of skeletal trauma before the butchering. Furthermore, the heads of the dogs were chopped into small, standardized 3-7 cm segmented pieces. This is in stark contrast to the cattle and the sheep/goats at the site which were butchered throughout the year, showing less intensive fragmentation. This was not a single event but occurred during several winters for at least two generations (Anthony & Brown 2017: 136-8, Brown & Anthony 2019: 97-100). Anthony & Brown (2017) interpret the ritualized treatment of the dog bones as an initiatory midwinter sacrifice for kóryos groups. The site shows similarity with midwinter ceremonies performed by the *vrātyas*, warrior dog-priests and kóryos equivalents, in the Rig Veda (Anthony 2007: 410).

Similar archaeological studies have used kóryos groups for top-down perspectives of socioeconomic developments. For instance, Kristiansen et al. (2017) hypothesize that kóryos members were one of the main agents behind the initial spread of PIE groups. This is because recent genetic, dietary, and archaeological evidence points to males being overrepresented during the early phase of expansion in the early Corded Ware horizon. The authors argue that the mobile and warlike character of kóryos groups could explain the predominately male IE expansion (Kristiansen et al. 2017: 338-40). Kristiansen (2022: 66) pursues this reasoning further, arguing that there were two important factors in the Yamnaya culture that acted as drivers for a male-centered expansion. The first was the organization of kóryos warbands, and the second was primogeniture, the principle that the eldest son inherits property. The second point would serve as a motivation for younger brothers in kóryos groups to establish their own settlements during their rite of passage periods outside of society rather than return. Furthermore, Kristiansen (2022: 66) argues that kóryos groups would later become institutionalized into chiefly retinues during the 2nd millennium BCE which ultimately gave rise to highly mobile warriors in the Bronze Age.

In the specific context of the NBA, Kaliff & Østigård (2022a) proposes a model of interpretation in which kóryos members were fundamental to NBA social organization and ritual based on a combination of textual sources, ethnographic accounts, folk beliefs, and archaeological contexts. With their connection to death, ancestor worship, and winter solstice, kóryos members ensured the continuation of social and cosmological order, along with the agricultural cycle. Kóryos would essentially be a ritual institution that trained young men to be warriors and leaders in both ritual and society (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 171-3, 198). The large NBA cairn Bredarör, located in Kivik, South Sweden, has always been assumed to be the burial of a king or a chief, based on its monumental size and the rock art slabs within. However, recent analyses show that the tomb contained six individuals, five of whom were teenagers. They were deposited over a 600-year period, between 1400 and 800 BCE, with about one burial per century. These teenage remains are argued to be kóryos members and would-be leaders who died prematurely during initiation. The youths of a kóryos could be the elite, mobile raiders and traders of the NBA, indicating both their socioeconomic and cosmological importance (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 31-8, 199ff, 215ff).

Archaeologists have had a very contentious approach to historical linguistics, often completely ignoring linguistic evidence when discussing issues such as the spread of IE languages (e.g. Renfrew 1987). This is a practice that has started to change in the 21st century, as more archaeologists turn to IE studies for interpretations (Kristiansen & Kroonen 2023: 5). This thesis situates itself in the growing field of IE archaeolinguistics such as those studies made by Anthony & Brown (2017) and Kaliff & Østigård (2022a). In a similar vein, this thesis aims to provide a new interpretive model for understanding the NBA, particularly rock art. As of yet, no study of kóryos based on NBA rock art has been made.

### 1.4. Theory

#### 1.4.1. Ideology

In this section, I present the concept of ideology and discuss its meaning and use within this thesis. Specifically, I aim to highlight how ideology plays an active role in shaping social relations and reproducing power structures.

The BA saw the rise of professionalized warriors as well as warrior aristocracies in Europe. Warriors and warfare are intrinsically linked to the mode of production at the time (Horn & Kristiansen 2018: 1, Kristiansen & Earle 2022: 18-20). As Earle (1997: 106) states, for warfare to be effective for political power in pre-state societies, it must also be controlled by economic or ideological constraints. To study warriorship in this period is thus also to study ideology. Similarly, there is a clear power dimension to kóryos groups, as the members typically consisted of the sons of elite families (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 44). The kóryos warband was, as Kershaw (2000: 18) argues, "as much religious as it was martial", and religion, as Lincoln (2018: 23) argues, is "the most ancient form of ideology". There is thus a clear connection between kóryos groups, warriorship, violence, power, and ritual. This makes ideology a suitable concept for analyzing kóryos, rock art, and their role in reproducing the socioeconomic structure of the NBA.

Defining ideology is difficult, as it has been used in various ways since its first coinage (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 15). In much archaeological research, the concept of ideology has been used interchangeably with beliefs or values in cultures. The term simply denotes all things related to the mental faculty, such as worldviews and ideas. However, this use of the term obscures the nuance involved in ideological construction and relations of power, thus making the concept lose its analytical power (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 40-5). In the following discussion, I will problematize this understanding of the concept and highlight its theoretical usefulness within my analysis.

The concept has a long history of use within the Marxist tradition, dating to Marx and Engels's book *Die deutsche Ideologie*, written between 1845 and 1846. Marx and Engels argue that ideology should not be seen as an identifiable set of beliefs or ideas. Rather, it is a distortion between social reality and its representation. A so-called "false consciousness" in which people are deceived into thinking in certain ways. The purpose of which is to misrepresent rulers' interests as the interests of all (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 17, Cranston 2023, Žižek 2008: 24). In more recent archaeological discourse, ideology is similarly defined as the distortion of social inequality and rationalization of exploitation (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 56). The commoners and non-elites are fooled into believing a false consciousness, most succinctly defined by Marx, according to Žižek, as: "They do not know it, but they are doing it" (Žižek 2008: 24). Since Marx and Engels, several theorists have problematized this line of reasoning and nuanced our understanding of ideology. These later theorists move away from the equation of ideology with false consciousness. Individuals are not simply duped by ideological distortions, masking reality "as it really is". Nor is ideology only located within people's minds. Rather, they argue that ideology is fundamentally material.

Antonio Gramsci was an important early scholar who explored the concept of ideology within Marxist theory. For Gramsci, an elite minority can only sustain their power in the long term by gaining the consent of the ruled (Crehan 2002: 99-101). Consent should not be viewed as a false consciousness or a normative internalized acceptance. Rather, this consent comes from the prestige that the dominant group holds within relations of production (Therborn 2012: 172-6). Ideological hegemony is produced when the dominated are co-opted into cultural networks that serve the interests of the elite (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 30). However, this co-optation and consent is not an idealized distortion of reality (Crehan 2002: 167). Rather, it is always materially embedded in practical activity. Ideology is the fluid and contextual nexus of material praxis, social relations, and ideas that produce, justify, and normalize social inequality, entangled in a web of coercion and consent (Crehan 2002: 101, 172-4, Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 28-30).

Gramsci can thus be seen as the transition from the older meaning of ideology, as false consciousness, to understanding it as materialized and embodied within social practice. The later Marxist Louis Althusser inherited this Gramscian notion of ideology to analyze ideological praxis (Eagleton 2012: 197). For Althusser (2012), ideology is always materialized in socioeconomic institutions, such as long-distance trade and rock art, as well as in the actions of everyday life. Althusser argues that since individuals are born into a name, language, and culture, they come into this world as already-made subjects. This subjectification of an individual is embodied in social practice, which makes ideology tangible and real in a material sense (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 52). Ideology is thus inherently performative and always rooted in social praxis (Eagleton 2012: 218ff, Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 31ff).

One of the biggest modern theorists to explore the concept of ideology is Slavoj Žižek. Although he takes inspiration from Althusser, Žižek moves away from the strict structuralism of Althusser's work. Žižek gives more agency to individuals, arguing that subjectification occurs in the dialectic between ideological performativity and a disidentification to ideology. Subjects do not wholesale internalize ideology, there is always a form of detachment from it (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 38ff). For Žižek, ideology lay not in the knowing, rather it lies in the doing. Oftentimes, individuals will doubt or even outright reject ideological claims. Yet, materially and practically, they still act as if they believe it. This doing despite our knowing, is what constitutes ideology, and the practical act generates conviction (Žižek 2008: 24-30, Myers 2003: 66-9). This ideological ritual, Žižek points out, creates the mode in which reality is conceptualized, as the ideological fantasy structures real material and social relations (Žižek 2008: 31-38, Myers 2003: 67).

But why do people acquiesce to ideology? Why do they perform the actions? Besides coercive methods, customs, and habits (Žižek 2008: 27, 34-6), feelings of sublimity incline people to submit. The sublime feeling of something much larger than the individual, like religion or the state, causes people to embrace an ideological fantasy due to its sheer overpowering aspect (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 40). Indeed, as Lincoln (2018: 23ff) argues, religions are particularly powerful ideologies, as they mask ideological claims as transcendent truths beyond debate. Myths and rituals, like ideologies, should thus be analyzed within their practical and material contexts, rather than from an idealistic perspective (Lincoln 2018: 24).

A commonly cited critique of ideology is the work by Abercrombie et al. (1980). They contend that while there is a dominant ideology, it only serves to integrate the elite. It doesn't create willing submission within the non-elite (Bernbeck & McGuire 2011: 32). However, the problem is that Abercrombie et al. conflate ideology with normative beliefs (Therborn 2012: 175). As I have argued in this chapter, ideology is not a normative and internalized belief that simply dupes the non-elite. Rather, ideology is an assemblage of coercion and consent materialized in objects and social practice. For Earle (1997: 151), the materialization of ideology is understood as the transformation of ideas and myths into physical forms, such as symbolic objects, monumental buildings, or rock art. However, in this definition, objects are only understood as passive representations of ideologies in material form. Rather, I argue that objects should be seen as actively co-constituting ideologies in social praxis. It is also important to stress that ideologies are not entirely coherent or consistent (Therborn 2012: 169). Ideologies are not dogmas. They create diverse patterns of cultural meaning that legitimize control. These patterns are not a source of power. Instead, they create a knowledge of power, both within the elite and the non-elite, that structures the actions of everyday life (Earle 1997: 144). Ideology in this thesis is thus understood as the social actions, ideas, and objects that consciously or unconsciously reproduce power structures. Kóryos members, as part of the elite, were both subjects to, and the driving force of, ideology.

#### 1.4.2. Rock Art

In the next three sections, I will present a brief research history of rock art interpretations followed by a discussion on the theoretical framework used within this thesis. Specifically, the "secret societies" model of NBA society argued for by Ling et al. (2018a, 2022), wherein elite groups of maritime and mobile warriors and traders used exclusionary and esoteric ritual practices to consolidate political control, serves as my main theoretical framework for interpreting rock art. The secret societies model is also argued to share similarities with kóryos, thus strengthening the connection between kóryos and the NBA. Furthermore, I argue that rock art should be viewed as a magical form of ideology used by this maritime elite to accentuate and reproduce certain social orders (Ling & Cornell 2010, Ling & Toreld 2018). In this view, rock art acted as a "model of", and a "model for" social reality (Lincoln 2014: 23).

NBA rock art has a long and varied history of interpretations. Almgren (1927) and other prominent archaeologists in the 20th century viewed rock art as representational and symbolic depictions of a lost NBA cosmology. Ship images were viewed as symbolic cult ships connected to agricultural fertility rituals, reflecting an assumed peaceful and agrarian historical period (Ling 2008: 25-8, Vandkilde 2013a). Contemporaneous and later interpretations of anthropomorphic figures in rock art have followed the symbolic interpretation. The figures have been viewed as depictions of gods and other supernatural figures, or as materializations of oral culture and other narrative stories (Ling & Cornell 2010: 28, Vandkilde 2013a: 51, Skoglund et al. 2022). However, peaceful and agrarian interpretations should be viewed with skepticism. The majority of rock art scenes depict boats and armed figures carved on former shorelines. Weapon, skeletal, and strontium analyses also indicate that the BA was a highly violent and mobile period (Ling 2008, Horn & Kristiansen 2018, Vandkilde 2013a, Wahl & Price 2013). Furthermore, while rock art does seem to reflect some myths and cosmological ideas, it cannot be reduced to pure reflections of oral culture and stories. Rather, rock art acted as its own reproducible source of social knowledge (Cornell & Ling 2010: 77ff).

Before presenting how rock art will be theoretically interpreted in this thesis, I will first present the theory of secret societies, as formulated by Hayden (2018), and its use in NBA research, as formulated by Ling et al. (2018a, 2022). This is necessary as it will provide background on NBA society and warriorship, as well as contextualize the theoretical rock art framework in this thesis.

#### 1.4.3. Secret Societies

"Secret societies" is a theoretical concept developed by archaeologist Brian Hayden (2018) based on ethnographic accounts from the Americas, Africa, and Oceania. Hayden presents the presence of exclusive social organizations made up of elites that use ritual and economic control in the acquisition of power. They often controlled vast economic resources and wielded substantial political power within the community (Hayden 2018: 4ff). As Ling (2019: 600) states, "[t]hey were shaped and operated by a rudimentary aristocracy of aggrandizers that strived to secure advantages for themselves". The secret part of these secret societies was not the existence of the organization or who its members were. Most often, it would have been obvious who was and who was not part of it. The secret was the ritual knowledge its members would seem to possess (Hayden 2018: 7). The purpose of secret societies was ultimately ideological control and structural power (Hayden & Earle 2022: 411). There are a few important characteristics shared by most secret societies. For instance:

- They commonly occurred in transegalitarian- or chiefdom-type societies, where surplus could be produced and controlled (Hayden 2018: 13ff). They occurred cross-culturally in several ethnographic accounts of similar societies. As such, they could be expected to appear in prehistoric societies with similar social and economic structures, such as the NBA (Chacon et al. 2020: 77, Hayden 2018: 285).
- They are associated with special ritual places or structures in remote locations. These were meeting places reserved for secret meetings or rituals. These places were always strictly off-limits for non-members (Hayden 2018: 287ff). Furthermore, these remote locations were often associated with rock art, carved as part of initiation rituals or ceremonies (Chacon et al. 2020: 77-9).
- They claimed to possess secret ritual knowledge. This could be highlighted in public displays of supernatural power or secret knowledge passed on to initiates (Hayden 2018: 1). Ritual power was thus monopolized, and the control of ritual magic was used to increase political prestige and legitimacy of authority. This magic was often performed with ritual paraphernalia, such as masks, with power-animal symbology and features (Ling 2019: 600ff, Hayden 2018: 288-90).
- They used terror tactics, violence, and intimidation to subordinate non-members. They are often associated with warfare, slavery, and coercive forms of subjugation (Ling 2019: 601, Chacon et al. 2020: 79, Ling et al. 2018a: 159). The term "terrorist" has been used to describe secret society members in several ethnographic accounts (Hayden 2018: 21).
- They supplanted biological kinships by creating new, supra-kinship groups. These ingroups "foster conditions that allow members to forge and maintain bonds beyond biologically based kin networks by way of fictive kin ties" (Ling et al. 2018a: 158).
- They often organized supraregional, exclusive networks with other secret societies. This was facilitated by long-distance exchange and the trade and acquisition of prestige objects. These networks resulted in similar rituals and art styles over large geographical areas (Ling 2019: 600ff, Ling et al. 2022: 63, Hayden 2018: 290ff, Chacon et al. 2020: 79).

An important aspect of the secret societies framework is the critique of how power and ritual have been theorized to constitute each other. As Hayden (2018: 2ff) points out, archaeologists have been vague when discussing how rituals create political power. A communitarian or functionalist approach is often used to argue for an integrative function of ritual and cosmology within societies. Hayden (2018: 15ff, 354) argues that ritual was rather just one aspect out of many for political control by aggrandizing individuals. However, the claim that control of ritual leads to control of power is rather simplistic. Ritual control is a form of information control, but that, in itself, is not the basis of power. Neither is belief in the power of ritual (Hayden 2018: 351ff). Graeber (2001: 86) argues that justifications were often not believed in stateless societies when it comes to the appropriation of surpluses, either by the non-ruling class or the elite. Indeed, as Hayden (2018: 18) points out, the acceptance of ritual claims by non-members was often due to more coercive methods, rather than any internalized actual belief. Ritual knowledge does not necessarily need to be believed for it to hold power. Much like ideology, people only need to be persuaded or coerced enough to conform to it (Hayden 2018: 352).

#### 1.4.4. Secret Societies, Rock Art, and Ideology

The secret societies framework has been used by Ling et al. (2018a, 2022) to interpret archaeological contexts in the NBA, particularly rock art. Based on archaeological finds and a comparative perspective, they hypothesize that an elite segment of NBA society that consisted of warriors, traders, ritual specialists, and boat-building craftsmen secured political control by engaging in long-distance exchange to obtain metals and raiding expeditions to obtain slaves. These elite groups functioned as secret societies that appropriated agro-pastoral surpluses and controlled access to trade, ritual, and warfare through coercion, debt, and ideological claims. Rock art is hypothesized to have been made by, and depict, these maritime groups during esoteric rituals connected to long-distance travels, raids, and initiations (Chacon et al. 2020, Ling & Toreld 2018: 74).

The term "magic" is applied to understand the praxis and motifs of rock art. Magic is understood as an active process intended to alter the social world and cause desired practical results through ritual and artistic means. In this sense, rock art may have functioned as a magical device intended to influence specific outcomes during secret society rituals (Ling & Cornell 2010: 33, Ling & Toreld 2018: 64, Fahlander 2019). For instance, increasing amounts of warfare-related motifs during certain periods correlate to an increase in the circulation of metals, indicating a connection between rock art, long-distance exchange, and raids (Ling et al. 2018a: 150). The limited choice of rock art motifs, and their specific positions in the landscape, indicates that they not only represented specific social actions but may have also served as magical devices intended to bring about practical results (Fahlander 2018: 380, 2019). Many rock art pieces were updated and recarved several times over long periods (Milstreu 2017, Bertilsson 2017a). This may indicate a form of magical "re-activation" of the motifs to strengthen their agential force.

Experimental archaeological studies indicate that the production of rock art was a long and tedious process that required extensive technical knowledge. Furthermore, the relatively uniform manner in which motifs were made and combined indicates that carvers possessed specialized aesthetic knowledge and conformed to certain codes. This suggests that rock art was created by a small group of specialized individuals (Ling 2008: 165ff). Indeed, Ling et al. (2018a: 166-8) argue that ritual specialists in secret societies were the ones who carved rock art during rituals intended to ensure the success of long-distance travels and raids or during initiation rites. The meaning of the motifs would have only been understood by members and initiates who possessed secret ritual knowledge.

Rock art was thus intimately connected to both the activities and rituals of secret societies. As such, it represented esoteric and magical knowledge as well as social praxis associated with maritime activities and warfare. Rock art served to magically manifest certain maritime and war-related ideologies, acting as both a "model of" and a "model for" specific social realities that its carvers wanted to reproduce. In this sense, rock art depicts a social reality, but an idealized one at that (Ling & Cornell 2010: 40, Lincoln 2014: 23). Rock art is thus understood to function both as ideological representations of kóryos rituals and as magical means to influence the rituals' desired outcome.

The secret societies framework is based on cross-cultural comparisons and an interdisciplinary theoretical approach intended to explore how NBA groups organized themselves. Specifically, a focus on the maritime sphere, long-distance exchange, rock art, and warriorship is shared by both secret societies in the ethnographic record and NBA societies. This makes the model suitable to apply and test in NBA contexts (Ling et al. 2018: 159-63, Chacon et al. 2020: 75ff). As Lincoln (2018: 21ff) predicts, comparative approaches will see a future resurgence within the human sciences, but unlike in earlier research, the object of study must be considered within its historical context for the approach to be fruitful. Because of this, the historical particularity of the NBA will be stressed in this thesis when interpreting rock art motifs in the secret societies and kóryos frameworks.

Secret societies were often linked to warriorship (Hayden & Earle 2022: 402). They often resembled military fraternities wherein warfare and ritual would constitute each other (Hayden 2018: 10ff, Chacon et al. 2020: 76). This makes the secret societies theory suitable for NBA rock art and kóryos, which both have strong associations with warriorship and the elite. Unlike Ling et al., I use the kóryos framework to explore the specific praxis and rituals of secret societies in the NBA. Kóryos can be interpreted within this theoretical framework due to similar connections to elites, exclusivity, access to esoteric knowledge, and violence. Poetry, a reoccurring kóryos attribute, was likewise connected to secrecy and magic in most IE contexts (Watkins 1995: 179-81, Jamison & Brereton 2014: 23).

### 1.5. Methodology

#### 1.5.1. Ideal Types

The material in this thesis will be analyzed through a comparative method. Specifically, these comparisons will be made using the concept of "ideal types". Originally formulated by sociologist Max Weber, ideal types are defined as "an attempt to analyze historically unique configurations of their individual components by means of generic concepts" (Weber 1949: 93). Ideal types are constructed theoretical concepts. They do not represent empirical reality exactly, but they are constructed from empirical data to serve as models for interpretation (Kaliff & Østigård 2020: 97). Ideal types should not be understood as normative for a phenomenon. They are not ideals in which the empirical data should strive as closely as possible. Rather, ideal types are understood as objects of comparison. They are used to compare and group different forms of empirical data to understand their relations. Ideal types are neither strict definitions of concepts. Rather, they should be seen as examples of how empirical material could be interpreted based on a set of characteristics. These characteristics are ultimately based on what is deemed important as defined by the research aim at hand. This allows the researcher to interpret certain material based on how closely it resembles the ideal type (Jansson Boström 2020: 71ff, 210).

For my analysis and other IE approaches to archaeology, the concept of ideal types is a useful methodological tool. For instance, ideal types have been used to interpret IE horse sacrifices in previous archaeological research (Kaliff & Østigård 2020). A similar approach will be used in this thesis. A kóryos ideal type will be constructed based on textual and linguistic evidence and interpreted within an archaeological context. It is important to stress that kóryos is not a unified phenomenon, and we should not expect to find a "pure" kóryos group that contains all elements (Kaliff & Østigård 2020: 97, Kershaw 2000: 180). Mythological themes and historical accounts from regions as far away as Ireland, Greece, and India should not be seen as direct examples of how kóryos functioned in ritual and practice. However, "[w]hat is important is the underlying structures that emanate from a common Indo-European heritage" (Kaliff & Østigård 2020: 39). Traditions change over time, but traditions may also contain traces of their original manifestation. This is the case with many IE themes that stem from their shared origin. All elements included in the kóryos ideal type may not represent the exact historic core of the original PIE ritual, but it does represent a thematic core shared by all its descendants (Kaliff & Østigård 2020: 26-8).

#### 1.5.2. Comparative Method and Indo-European Studies

A systematic comparison between kóryos-like groups shows that several characteristics are shared throughout a wide spatial and temporal range (Kershaw 2000, West 2007). Although we lack textual accounts of the NBA, kóryos groups may be assumed to have existed in some capacity. This is assumed based on its common occurrence in most other IE-speaking groups, highlighting its deeply embedded ritual structure most likely originating in PIE-speaking groups. Although all manifestations contain a common core, there are cultural variations that make each iteration unique. As Kaliff & Østigård (2022b: 3-4) state:

> The great strength of interdisciplinary Indo-European studies is precisely that, because of a shared core Indo-European origin, it focuses on common structures and cultural features that are possible to identify across other differing cultural, religious, geographical and temporal variables and variabilities. Thus, in many cases, the core and origin will not be the most interesting, but the distribution, continuity, and consequences of the very different and multifaceted Indo-European processes.

The lack of textual sources makes the reconstruction of a uniquely NBA kóryos group difficult. This is where archaeological contexts are useful. By heuristically comparing the constructed kóryos ideal type with rock art motifs, I explore how kóryos manifested and functioned within its regional peculiarity. Simultaneously, the kóryos ideal type serves to explore the socioeconomic development of the NBA and its primary causes.

### 1.6. Primary Sources

In this section, I present and date the most important sources in my thesis. This includes textual sources, iconographic motifs, and NBA rock art. In Chapter 2, I use a wide and varied array of sources such as historical accounts and myths to construct a kóryos ideal type, covering a temporal range of 4000 BCE to 1300 CE and a spatial range of India to Ireland. Specifically, I have chosen material based on two criteria:

1. The material is made by, or associated with, individuals and groups attested, or assumed, to speak an Indo-European language. This includes groups and contexts associated with the Indo-Iranian, Greek, Italic, Celtic, Germanic, Balto-Slavic, and Hittite branches of the IE language family.
2. The material describes, depicts, or pertains to attributes associated with kóryos groups in previous research. This includes a focus on wolves, dogs, werewolves, death, warfare, warbands, elites, liminality, initiations, male youths, magic, poetry, weapon dancing, spears, nudity, and ecstatic fury.

The material is chosen if it contains several of these attributes together and if it bears similarity to material from other contexts.

Due to the large number of textual sources used in my analysis, I focus on the ones most frequently used in each major IE context. Lesser used sources will instead be dated and contextualized within the analysis. Furthermore, lesser used contexts such as Balto-Slavic and Hittite will not be presented in this section. Following the textual sources, I present NBA rock art, specifically focusing on the dating and landscape context of rock art.

#### 1.6.1. Textual Sources

The earliest textual sources used in this thesis come from the Indo-Iranian context. Specifically, the Rig Veda written in Vedic Sanskrit, and the Avesta or Gathas written in Old Avestan are used. Both texts are liturgical compilations of praise hymns and ritual performances, most likely composed around 1400 to 1000 BCE (Jamison & Brereton 2014: 3-5, Watkins 1995: 57). Other early sources include the two Homeric poems the Iliad and Odyssey, written in early Greek and dated close to 800 BCE (Powell 2007: 52). Italic sources, notably Latin, describing the Lupercalia festival come from Cicero around the 1st century BCE and Plutarch around the 1st century CE (Anthony & Brown 2017: 135ff, Vukovic 2018: 38). Mentions of the kóryos-like *iuvenes* and *Salii* by writers such as Vergil and Livy are dated to around the same period (Kershaw 2000: 136, Woodard 2012).

An important source for early Germanic contexts is *Germania*, written in 98 CE by the Roman historian Tacitus. *Germania* was written as an ethnographic account detailing the organization, religion, and customs of a group of tribes called *Germani* living north of the Roman Empire (Schjødt 2020: 257). Although this account is undoubtedly biased, written by Tacitus as a polemic against what he considered the moral degeneracy of contemporary Romans, many scholars today recognize that his account contains traces of real information concerning Germanic groups (O'Gorman 2012, Meier 1999, Schjødt 2020: 257-65). Partially, this is due to similarities between Tacitus' descriptions of Germanic warbands and later sources from the 1st millennium CE up to the Viking Age (Harris 2008: 294-6). Skaldic poetry and sagas from Norse contexts, dated from the early 10th century to the 14th century CE, are also used in my analysis. Despite many of them having been written after the Christianization of Scandinavia, similarities with earlier texts point to a continued, albeit garbled, oral tradition containing traces of pre-Christian rituals (Schjødt 2020: 573-83).

Textual sources relating to Celtic contexts are a bit more varied, covering the period of the 3rd century BCE to the 13th century CE. The earliest of these are written by Roman and Greek authors, such as Livy, Polybius, and Julius Caesar who refer to the inhabitants of West Europe and the British Isles as Gauls. Like Tacitus, these authors relied on second-hand accounts of Celtic people, and their reports contain political generalizations (Faszcza 2018: 151-3, Cunliffe 2018: 6-13). Irish sources dated to the 7th to 13th century CE, with a focus on the 8th to 10th century, are also used as part of the Celtic cultural sphere. This includes accounts by Christian monks writing polemically against non-Christian practices, as well as early medieval law texts (Arbuthnot & Parsons 2012: 9, McCone 2012: 14-9). Similarities and reoccurring attributes in these sources indicate that both early Greek and Roman authors, as well as later Irish texts, describe a shared kóryos tradition partially obscured by different political and religious biases (McCone 2012: 20-7).

Textual sources in this thesis will be approached with caution, as many of them possess source-critical issues. For instance, several of them are written by individuals outside the cultural context they aim to describe. As mentioned in the previous sections, none of these sources should be seen as direct examples of how kóryos functioned. Rather, the material has been chosen and valued based on the occurrence of kóryos attributes. Migration, contact, and societal developments ensured that kóryos groups were shaped by differing historical trajectories. However, some attributes are more prevalent than others and they continually reappear in different contexts. If several sources, separated spatially and temporally, describe and associate these similar attributes in similar ways, then that may reflect traces of a shared tradition going back to PIE groups, rather than sheer coincidence. Textual sources will thus not be taken as literal. Rather, they are used to highlight similar traces of a shared kóryos tradition present in many IE contexts. The attributes will be synthesized into a semi-sequential order in Chapter 2, focusing on the social praxis and rituals of kóryos groups.

#### 1.6.2. NBA Rock Art

Rock art in Scandinavia can be divided into two cultural traditions: the Northern Scandinavian tradition and the Southern Scandinavian tradition. The former is older, traditionally dated to the Neolithic (4000-1700 BCE), and largely associated with hunter-gatherer groups in North Scandinavia (Melheim & Ling 2017, Bertilsson 2017b: 89ff). The Southern tradition covers a temporal range between the end of the late Neolithic to the Pre-Roman Iron Age (PRIA, 500-1 BCE). However, the overwhelming majority are dated to the NBA (Ling 2008: 1, Ling et al. 2018a: 152). The highest concentration of rock art in the Southern tradition is located on the west coast of Sweden and the east coast of Norway, as well as in Uppland and Norrköping in eastern Sweden, and Skåne in south Sweden. This rock art was predominantly carved on former shorelines and features boats as the most common motif, indicating a prominent maritime focus (Ling & Cornell 2015: 266, Ling 2012: 465). Most panels were carved in open and accessible areas, indicating that they may have been made for public viewing (Ling & Cornell 2017: 30). However, in many ethnographic accounts, the ritual locations of secret societies were considered taboo and dangerous for non-initiates to visit. This may have been the case for rock art locations during the NBA as well. This means that the location of these panels may only have been known to members (Chacon et al. 2020: 81). In this thesis, only Southern tradition rock art dated to the NBA will be covered. These carvings are referred to as "NBA rock art" or simply "rock art" for clarity.

In this thesis, I focus on motifs containing anthropomorphic figures that are depicted with weapons or appear in martial settings, referred to as warriors. Depictions of warriors and weapons are, like boats, common in NBA rock art. In fact, martial and violent motifs are more frequently depicted in rock art from the NBA compared to in any other region in BA Europe (Ling & Cornell 2017: 16). The earliest warrior motifs date to around 1500-1400 BCE, and they appear continually until 300 BCE with the majority dated between 1500 to 700 BCE. There are regional variabilities when it comes to the chronology. However, a large portion of warrior motifs were carved during two phases dated to 1500-1300 BCE and 900-700 BCE regardless of region. As previously mentioned, these phases correspond closely to the supply of metals in the NBA, with warrior motifs becoming more prevalent during periods of increased circulation. This points to a connection between warriors, trade, and the maritime sphere in the NBA (Ling & Cornell 2015:266, 2017: 22-4, Ling et al. 2018a: 150). The rock art used in this thesis is predominantly from Sweden and dates to the most common chronological period, around 1500 to 700 BCE. The motifs have specifically been chosen based on their correspondence to the kóryos ideal type.

## 2. Kóryos

Kóryos was not a static phenomenon. Just like any institution in prehistory, it was molded by differing historical trajectories set in motion by the initial PIE migrations throughout Eurasia. However, all subsequent descendants contain traces of the original warband. This is a summary of the attributes that will be explored in this chapter, which also serves as my ideal type:

- They were an all-male warband dedicated to raiding and warfare.
- Its members were ritually associated with dogs, wolves, and death. This association materialized in canine personal names and dress.
- They mostly contained adolescents with elite backgrounds, initiated into the band as age-sets.
- Members inhabited a liminal state, at times living outside of society figuratively and literally.
- They engaged in activities considered taboo by societal norms but socially sanctified if directed at other societies. This could include violence, cannibalism, and sexual assault.
- They engaged in ritual activities intended to incite a state of ecstatic fury, such as weapon dancing, chanting, and the use of psychoactive substances.
- Nudity played an important ritual role.
- They memorized and recited heroic poetry.
- Their patron gods often reflect the attributes listed above, such as a connection to the elite, warbands, magic, and poetry.

### 2.1. Etymology

A comparative approach reveals many different historical descendants of kóryos around Eurasia that share characteristics and etymologies. The reconstructed PIE word *kóryos* can be translated to "army, warband, unit of warriors" (Mallory 2006: 91). Compare with words in Celtic *cuire* "troop", Lithuanian *kãrias* "army", Old English *here* "army", Germanic *harja* "army", Greek *koíranos* "leader of the warband" (Mallory 2006: 91, Mallory & Adams 2006: 278, 282, West 2007: 449).

The term *kóryos* has never been attested in any textual source. However, kóryos groups have been attested under different names in different historical contexts. In a Vedic context, two types of kóryos are present. The *vrātyas* of the earthly realm, from *vrāta* "oath", and their celestial counterpart, the terror-spreading Maruts, who accompany the war god Indra as a troop (Falk 2002: 32, Kershaw 2000: 201, Jamison & Brereton 2014: 49ff). In a broader Indo-Iranian context, members are also called *marya* or *maiyra* "young man, villain" (McCone 1987: 119). The terms are cognate with the name of the Maruts and the Roman war god Mars, from the root *mr-* "death" (Kazanas 2001: 277ff, Kershaw 2000: 217).

In a Celtic context, they are called *fían*, derived from \*wēd- "wild". However, this name seems to be a later replacement of the word *cuire* "troop" to denote kóryos bands (McCone 2012: 20ff). The Celtic groups *Tricorii* and *Petrucorii* mentioned in Roman sources were probably alliances consisting of three and four kóryos bands (West 2007: 449).

Although there is no Homeric attestation of kóryos groups, the term for a leader of one survives in *koíranos*, *kóryos* with the suffix *-nos* "leader of" (Kershaw 2000: 11). The word *epikouros* "ally", from *kouros* "young man" might also be connected to the practice (Bremmer 2021: 88). The later Ephebes institution of Classical Greece and Spartan Agoge training may be descendants of kóryos with more social inclusion, involving all youths of society (von Cieminski 2002: 93-105). In Italic, the young *iuvenes* troops, led by Romulus and Remus, lived in the wild and conducted raids (McCone 1986: 20, Kershaw 2000: 135). The *lupreci*, from *lupus* "wolf" and the *Salii*, dancing priests of Mars dressed as archaic warriors, retain many ritual aspects of the warband but without the focus on warfare (Meiser 2002: 4, Tennant 1988, Bailey & North 2005).

The Germanic term is harder to elucidate, as several names for warbands are found. For instance, Tacitus calls the Germanic warrior retinue *comitas* (Harris 2008: 301). The later Old Norse word describing the same concept is *drótt* "retinue", as well as the broader term *herr* "army" from Proto-Germanic \*harjaz "army, warrior". \*Harjaz is most likely the indigenous term for a kóryos band (Schjødt 2020: 561-73, Hartmann & Riegger 2022: 50, McCone 1987: 111). The latter term is earliest attested around 55 BCE on the Negau B helmet found in Slovenia, with the Germanic words \*harjagastiz teiwaz "War(rior)-guest god" inscribed in Rhaetic script (Markey 2001). The *Harii*, described by Tacitus as a tribe, were most likely a special warband part of a larger Germanic army. Likewise, the *berserkir* "bear- or bare-shirt" and *úlfhéðnar* "wolf-skins" of Norse sagas were special warrior groups set apart from other retinues (Schjødt 2020: 561, 572-4, McCone 1987: 106).

### 2.2. Life in the Kóryos

The PIE kóryos warband consisted of unmarried young males who were assorted and initiated into age-sets. The age of the initiates varied depending on the context. However, initiation would generally follow early adolescence (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 44, Mallory 2006: 93). In Spartan society, *agoge* training started as early as seven years old (von Cieminski 2002: 98). In most other contexts, however, initiates were in their early teens, and kóryos served as the stage between childhood and adulthood. For instance, in Celtic law, childhood ends at the age of 14 but entering adulthood, with the legal right to own property and marry, was not possible until the age of 20. Between these ages, one was considered a *fer midbad* "man of middle" which characterized the kóryos period (McCone 2012: 16-8). Similarly, later Vedic tutelage involved a four-year liminal period of living in the wild between the ages of 16 and 20 (Kershaw 2000: 204). In Ancient Persia, a *yuvān mart* "young man" was someone between the ages 15 and 20 (McCone 1987: 125). The Germanic sources do not mention any specific age where this transitional period begins. However, Caesar writes that marriage is frowned upon before the age of 20 in Germanic society (McCone 1986: 19). Initiation and entrance into a kóryos group is generally described to occur during early adolescence. This can be seen in Tacitus' description of the Harii and Taifali (McCone 2012: 27, Weiser 1927: 42ff). For a later Norse context, in *Hrólfs saga kraka*, Hjalti is initiated into a retinue of *berserkir* as a young adolescent. In the *Völsunga saga*, Sinfjötli is ten years old when he begins his initiation as an *úlfhéðinn* (Schjødt 2020: 576-9). Taken together, kóryos members likely began their initiation sometime in early adolescence, between the age of ten to 15 years old with a probable cut-off point around 20. This marked a five- to ten-year-long period of wolf-like existence in the liminal warband. One may assume that the group possessed a senior instructor who taught them fighting tactics, poetry, and rituals at certain times during this period. For instance, an older warrior acting as a mentor is common in Germanic and Celtic contexts (Enright 1996: 203, McCone 2012: 28ff, Harris 2008: 299).

The initial ritual, indicating the shift from childhood into a kóryos state, is largely unexplored. However, it often involves undergoing ritual death in which new identities are assumed (Harris 2008: 304, McCone 1986: 49ff). Historical and archaeological sources indicate that this first initiation ritual could involve members sacrificing and dismembering dogs or wolves, the mock killing of a wild animal, and drinking the blood of a wolf, an enemy, or each other (Anthony & Brown 2017, Kershaw 2000: 143, Schjødt 2020: 577). In some contexts, a leader would be chosen among the initiates. In Celtic society, the leader was chosen by passing certain martial tests (McCone 1986: 7). For the Vedic *vrātyas*, it was determined by a dice game (Falk 1986: 188). This ritual, and subsequent rituals throughout the kóryos period, occurred during seasonal events depending on geographical context. In India, they followed the monsoon rains. Further north, however, they occurred in winter, especially during the winter solstice (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 17, Kershaw 2000: 122ff).

The size of a kóryos band seems to vary. Numbers such as three, five, or nine appear frequently, but the most common number is groups of twelve, appearing often in Germanic, Celtic, and Roman contexts. Larger groups of 50, and even 150, members also appear frequently (Kershaw 2000: 126-8, McCone 1986: 13). It may be, as McCone (1987: 109) argues, that smaller kóryos groups could come together or divide at will depending on their task. Indeed, as King Cormac of Irish saga, a *fían* member in his youth, tells his son:

> I used to kill a pig, I used to follow a track when I was alone; I used to march against a band [...] of five when I was one of five; I was ready for slaughter when I was one of ten; I was ready for raiding when I was one of twenty; I was ready for battle when I was one of a hundred. These were my deeds (McCone 2012: 20).

However, smaller groups were essentially the standard, most likely consisting of five to twelve individuals.

Upon initiation, members received a weapon and equipment to mark the transition from childhood to kóryos. In the Vedic sources, initiates were given a mace and leather belt that represented loyalty to the group (Lincoln 1981: 128). However, in most other contexts, the weapon of choice was a spear. In Greece, Attic Ephebes initiates were given a spear and a shield (von Cieminski 2002: 95). Spears are also very common among Homeric warriors (West 2007: 460). The Roman *Salii* dressed as archaic warriors and carried a spear, shield, and sword (Bailey & North 2005). Germanic youths were given a spear and a shield upon adolescence according to Tacitus (Schjødt 2020: 569). Irish *fían* members were, much the same, given a spear and a shield during initiation. This kit, including a belt, is also present among descriptions of Celts in Roman sources (McCone 2012: 18, 27).

Finally, the members would pledge allegiance to their patron god, leader, and each other, becoming bound together in an oath-brotherhood. The initiates subsequent years were to be defined by new identities, activities, and rituals, which will be explored below.

#### 2.2.1. Wolves and Death

An association between warriors and certain animals is a common cross-cultural phenomenon. Ethnographic material indicates that warriors commonly associated themselves with predatory animals such as bears, hawks, lions, and sharks, especially within secret societies (Hayden 2018: 150, 210, Lincoln 1981: 163). A connection between warriors and canines is not uniquely IE either. For instance, warriors of the Cheyenne and Crow tribes of North America identified themselves with dogs and wolves (Reeder 2001, Lincoln 1991: 145). In this section, I will present the intimate association between wolves and warriorhood that characterized the identity and praxis of kóryos groups. This association sustained itself through a particularly strong connection between canines, death, lawlessness, liminality, and raiding common in many IE contexts.

Kóryos members seasonally lived in the wild outside of society. They engaged in taboo behavior and were trained to kill. Similar to a wolf, they always moved in groups as a pack (Kershaw 2000: 176ff). It is not surprising then that kóryos groups and warriors were frequently ascribed wolf-like names and qualities. In fact, warrior youths associated with wolves occur in Celtic, Germanic, Italic, Greek, Indo-Iranian, and Balto-Slavic sources (McCone 1986: 21). For instance, these warriors were able to ritually transform themselves into wolves by wearing wolf skins (Lincoln 1981: 126). The *úlfhéðnar* of Norse contexts were warriors and warbands associated with wolves. They were often mentioned in connection with the *berserkir* - often interchangeably - and both are described as wearing wolf skins. *Héðinn* means a hooded cloak covering the head (Simek 1993: 338, Kershaw 2000: 60, 157ff). They howled and shook their spears before battle, just like the *berserkir* who bit their shields, roared, and became mad like wolves in war (Sundqvist & Hultgård 2004: 589, Kershaw 2000: 44ff). The Anglo-Saxons referred to Viking raiders as *wælwulfas* "slaughter-wolves", which may refer to one or several troops of *úlfhéðnar* (Price 2019: 304-6). *Úlfhéðnar* shares similarities with the young *velites* of the Roman army who also wore wolf hides and carried spears (Price 2019:310). The dog-headed cannibalistic warriors among the Langobards also fit the description. In addition, the former name of the Langobards was *Winnili*, from German *winnig* "howling, barking" (Oettinger 1992: 430, Kershaw 2000: 142ff). The most famous *úlfhéðnar*, Sigmundr and his son Sinfjötli, transformed into howling wolves and, in a rage, killed several men after putting on wolf hides. They eventually cooled off and removed their wolf clothes prior to their return to society (Schjødt 2020: 580, Price 2019: 302). In Irish sources, *mac tire*, "sons of the land" was a term used to identify both wolves and warriors living in the wild (McCone 1985: 171-4). The practice of youth warbands raiding other societies was known as "wolfing", in the "form of wolves". This is connected to the word *luchthonn* "wolf-skin", referring to the garb of the warriors (McCone 1986: 15ff). In Italic sources, Romulus, leader of an *iuvenes* troop, wore a wolf skin, and Caeculus' retinue of young men armed with spears and slings also wore head coverings made of wolf hide (Kershaw 2000: 136, 166). The wolfishness of a kóryos group survives in the *Iliad* with the Myrmidons who prepare for battle by acting as wolves and consuming raw meat, and the lightly clad Lykians from *lykos* "wolf" (Lincoln 1981: 126ff, Cebrián 2010: 343-6). The Lykians, however, wore goat skins according to Homer and Herodotus (Cebrián 2010: 348ff). This is similar to Indo-Iranian kóryos members, who wore blackened sheep skins, but they might originally have been wolf skins, and a leather belt (Kershaw 2000: 202). They were known *vŕka* "wolves" or *bizangranam vŕka* "two-legged wolves" (Lincoln 1981: 125ff).

Personal names containing "wolf" or "dog" are also commonly attested throughout the IE world. Some examples include Indo-Iranian *Vŕkajina* "Wolf skin" and *Dasyave Vŕka* "Barbarian wolf" (West 2007: 450, Lincoln 1981: 126). Many Greek heroes possess wolf-like names, such as Lykos, Lykurgos, and Autolykus (Kershaw 2000: 162-4). Celtic names such as Cenn Fáelad "Wolf head", Lochán "Wolf", and the common formation Cú "hound" are also related. In fact, a recorded leader of a *fían* band was named Fáelán "Little wolf" (McCone 1986: 9, 16). Wolf names are particularly common in Germanic contexts - Wolfhetan "Wolf skin", Herfólfr "Army wolf", Wulfila "Little wolf", and the *berserkir* Úlfr "Wolf" to name a few (McCone 1987:104, Kershaw 2000: 157). Kóryos members might have retained their wolf-like names even after their reintegration into society. For instance, the *berserkir* Kveldulf "Evening wolf" keeps his name despite his retirement from warriorship (McCone 1987: 104). Likewise, Sundqvist & Hultgård (2004) argue that a group of 6th century CE rune stones from South Sweden describing three generations of men with the names *Hapuwulfr* "Battle wolf", *Hariwulfr* "Army wolf", and *Heruwulfr* "Sword wolf" refer to local elites who were part of a kóryos group earlier in life. Kóryos members may have received wolf-like names as a part of their new identity during initiation. Names that would identify them in adulthood as former members.

There is a general connection between wolves, death, and criminality present in most IE contexts, and it is hard to determine whether or not this is due to the kóryos connection, or vice versa. The connection between wolves and death is significantly older than the initial PIE cultures (Brown & Anthony 2019: 8), and it has continued in folklore up until the modern period (Woods 1954). A common manifestation of this mytheme is the dog guardian of the underworld, such as Kerberos, found in most IE contexts (Lincoln 1991: 96). Wolves were metaphors for death, and they frequently appeared on battlefields as corpse-eaters. Similarly, in medieval Germanic and English law, a wolf denoted someone who unearths corpses in graveyards (Kershaw 2000: 179, 257, Lincoln 1991: 106, Price 2019: 283, Pluskowski 2023: 229). Furthermore, outlaws were often likened to wolves. Several words for wolves with ominous connotations can be traced to PIE times, such as \*ulkʷos "dangerous", \*ualos "woeful", and the root \*werg- "strangling, tearing" (Adams et al. 1997: 646, Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 59). The latter term can be traced from the Old Norse word *vargr* and Proto-Germanic \*wargaz meaning both wolf and outlaw, as well as the Hittite word \*hurkel "capital offence" (Kershaw 2000: 178). In Hittite law, to "become a wolf" means that one is legally and socially excluded from society after committing certain crimes. A forced marriage by abduction is similarly labeled a "wolf-marriage" (Weitenberg 1991: 192–5). In a Greek context, one becomes a wolf excluded from society after the consumption of human flesh during the Zeus Lykaios festival (McCone 1987: 121). A murderer in Norse sources was considered a *morðvargr* "murder-wolf". The Anglo-Saxons similarly referred to criminals as "wolf-heads" (Price 2019: 304–6, Sundqvist & Hultgård 2004: 591). Werewolves associated with magic and the dead continue in folklore throughout the Middle Ages in Europe, sharing many typological similarities with Norse *berserkir* and *úlfhéðnar* (Price 2019: 313). Identifying as a wolf thus carried more connotations than a penchant for raiding and fighting as a pack. It implied a liminal existence in a state of wilderness. It implied immoral behavior, violence, and a connection to death.

#### 2.2.2. Sons of Kings

A common attribute found among the members of a kóryos group is that they often consist of adolescent males from the upper social stratum of society. They are often described as the sons of kings, chiefs, and elites, or they are connected to the top of the social hierarchy in some way. In Irish sources, kóryos members are strongly associated with nobility. They are frequently referred to as *maic ríg* "sons of kings", *maic flaithi* "sons of nobles" and sometimes interchangeably *maic báis* "sons of death". Likewise, members mentioned by name, such as Fáelán, Conaire, and Ingcél are sons of kings (McCone 1986: 2–9, 1987: 105). In Vedic sources, kóryos members seem to come from high social status, as sons of kings and the priesthood (Heesterman 1962: 8–15). The warband was gradually subsumed into the Brahmin caste of later periods, which is considered the highest rank of the Indian caste system (Anthony & Brown 2017: 135, Sen 2022: 2). *Vrátya* would later refer to an uninitiated Brahmin rather than a warband (Falk 2002: 32). In Rome, the *Salii* only consisted of patricians (Bailey & North 2005). During the Lupercalia festival, the blood of a dog and a goat was smeared on the forehead of two adolescent males who represented two elite lineages (Anthony & Brown 2017: 136). Germanic warbands seem to have formed the elite of society, especially the retinue of a chief or king. Tacitus indicates that sons of the elite join the chief's retinue at a young age (Schjødt 2020: 254, 570ff). This is also reflected in the Gothic Passio S. Sabae, dated to the 4th century CE, wherein the Gothic chief Atharid travels with a retinue of "lawless brigands", one of whom is his son (Enright 1996: 203). Despite their bad reputation in Old Norse sagas, *berserkir* and *úlfhéðnar* often belong to families of high social standing. For instance, Arngrim, Vestmar, and their sons, as well as Egil Skallagrim's family all share a prominent social status (Kershaw 2000: 43ff).

The connection between kóryos and the elite is particularly prevalent in mythological origin stories of heroes and kings. It is common for legendary kings or other prominent figures in IE myth to be associated with wolves in their childhood and adolescence. A popular thematic variation involves a child with royal blood being raised by a she-wolf, leading a band of young outlaws engaged in raiding, and later becoming king. This mytheme is present in the origin stories of Remus and Romulus in Italic, Cyrus the Great in Persian, King Cormac in Irish, and Harald and Halvdan in Old Norse sources (Kershaw 2000: 135–9, McCone 2012: 20, 1987: 139). The *úlfhéðnar* Sigmundr and Sinfjötli are likewise the son and grandson of King Völsungr. After their wolfish episode and reintegration into society, Sigmundr becomes a king and Sinfjötli a warrior (Schjødt 2020: 579ff). The origin story of the Irish hero Cú Chulainn "Culann's dog" shares many similarities with Old Norse *úlfhéðnar* and *berserkir* initiations. He was initiated as a warrior in adolescence during which he received his canine name, a spear, and a shield. Following this, he went away and joined a *fían* band, where he hunted game and killed three men in a frenzied state. Upon returning home, Cú Chulainn had to be dunked in three barrels of cold water to cool off before his reintegration back into society (McCone 1986: 17). Other prominent leaders with a wolf connection include the Irish mythical king Cú Roi "dog of the battlefield" and Greek Lykos "wolf", founder of Lykia (Kershaw 2000: 159–162). Furthermore, the legendary founder of Spartan *agoge* training was named Lycurgus "man of wolf-deeds" (McCone 1986: 21). Many heroes, kings, and leaders are associated with wolves, despite the animal's connection to criminality and death. This most likely reflects an older connection between kóryos and elites present among PIE and post-PIE groups, as many heroic stories mirror kóryos rituals.

#### 2.2.3. Patron Gods

Upon initiation, kóryos members were not only oath-bound to their group, but also their patron god. Examined with a kóryos framework, certain attributes associated with certain gods in IE contexts most likely point to an older and reconstructable PIE kóryos-god. Schjødt (2020: 1127) argues that, at its most basic form, this PIE god was connected to youth warbands and ecstatic fury. However, other reoccurring structural patterns may predate at least the NBA.

The figures most discussed in association with kóryos groups are the Germanic Odin, Celtic Lug and Finn, Vedic Indra and Rudra, and the Iranian Mithra. They are often referred to because they were ascribed many kóryos-like attributes in textual sources, such as a connection with warbands, royalty, magic, and poetry. In a Greek and Italic context, the evidence is more fragmented as different kóryos attributes are associated with different gods (Kershaw 2000: 187-95). This may have occurred as the kóryos practice ceased to exist in these societies and new cultural contexts called for new gods. But some structural residues of a kóryos god remained.

The oldest references are Indo-Iranian. As previously mentioned, the *vrātyas* were the youth warband of Vedic society, while the Maruts were their celestial counterpart. The Maruts always appear as a troop and spread terror. No individual Marut is ever named, meaning that the troop is more important than any one member. Rudra is the father of the Maruts, and they are subsequently called *rudras* at times, although they frequently accompany Indra as a warband (Jamison & Brereton 2014: 49ff). They are frequently described as being the same age, indicating an age-set, and carrying spears or clubs as weapons. They are *sīrī* "powerful" and *ayās* "unruly, wild". Yet, they also appear as priests reciting hymns (Kershaw 2000: 214-9). This warrior-priest dichotomy is also reflected in descriptions of Indra. In the earliest Vedic texts, Indra is both a war leader and a god of poetry and hymns, but in later Vedic texts his role changes and he becomes a storm god (Jamison & Brereton 2014: 38ff). Upon initiation, *vrātyas* were given a belt that symbolizes loyalty to the group, and a mace, the weapon of Indra (Lincoln 1981: 128). Rudra, on the other hand, is a demonic and angry deity identified with the wild and death, but also healing. He is associated with dogs and his name may mean "Howler". Later Vedic texts also describe him as a warlord (Jamison & Brereton 2014: 53, Kershaw 2000: 210-23). Indra and Rudra's characteristics, taken together, share many similarities to kóryos groups as terror-spreading warriors who also engage in poetry and magic. The gods are unified in their connection to the Maruts, a deified kóryos band, and the two may have been one in pre-Vedic times (Das 2002: 149ff). Iranian god Mithra has elements of both deities. He was the god of royalty and war, but also described as mysterious and malicious. Early Mithra worship comprised ritual dancing and drinking by royalty (Kershaw 2000: 183).

Odin, despite being the most recent god mentioned by name, can be seen as an archetypical kóryos god. This is not to say that Odin's name, cult, or characteristics have not changed since PIE times, but he does retain many kóryos-like attributes that appear contradictory without the proper framework (Kershaw 2000: 69). Odin is said to be the all-father of gods and the leader of the æsir. He is the god of royalty and outcasts, war and death, but also poetry and magic (Price 2019: 55, Schjødt 2020: 1144-65). Odin is the patron of warbands and warriors, often imparting courage in battle to his chosen champions. He is the ancestor of numerous kings and warriors, such as the *úlfhéðnar* Sigmundr and Sinfjötli, to whom he grants occult knowledge (Schjødt 2020: 1165-94). More than 200 names of Odin can be found in Eddic and Skaldic poetry, which reflects his different aspects. The largest part of these are related to warfare, while most other names allude to wisdom, ecstatic fury, magic, and the dead (Price 2019: 62-68). One of these is *Herjann* "leader of the warband" from PIE \*koryonos, related to Homeric *koíranos* with the same meaning, indicating his close relation to warbands (Kershaw 2000: 11ff). The sagas seem to frequently infer that Odin is the protector of the elite and their retinues, especially *berserkir* and *úlfhéðnar* (Schjødt 2020: 577). Odin is similarly the god and leader of dead warriors. The *einherjar* are the slain kings and warriors that populate Valhǫll, a realm of dead fighters ruled by Odin. To warriors initiated to Odin, death was an invitation to join his retinue in the afterlife (Schjødt 2020: 1166ff, Kershaw 2000: 13ff). This may have survived in the later Germanic folklore phenomenon termed The Wild Hunt, wherein a ghostly troop led by Odin is said to appear during the Yule season. This concept may have been inspired by actual kóryos-like warbands, who themselves were connected to Odin, death, and the winter solstice (Simek 1993: 372ff, Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 163ff). He is associated with ravens and wolves, themselves symbols of death and battlefields (Price 2019: 60).

One of Odin's main attributes was his spear, Gungnir, which is associated with warband initiations, kings, and human sacrifice (Schjødt 2020: 1145, Enright 1996: 224). Up to eight spear-related names for Odin are recorded, such as *Geirsdróttinn* "Spear-Lord" (Price 2019: 62ff). Just as the *úlfhéðnar* shake their spears before battle, so is Odin called *Gungnirs vafðr* "Gungnir's shaker" (Enright 1996: 224). The spear was a strong indicator for Odin, and non-indigenous writers, such as Adam of Bremen, compared him to Mars as both carried spears (Schjødt 2020: 1178). Spears are a common element in many rituals dedicated to Odin, such as blood brotherhood initiations. Odinic warriors were to be marked or pierced by a spear upon death. Similarly, one could dedicate an enemy or a buried individual to Odin by throwing a spear over them (Price 2019: 95, Schjødt 2020: 1168). The Langobardic king Agilmund was said to be the offspring of the Gugingus, most likely referencing Gungnir (Hedeager 2011: 43), indicating the spear's ritual significance in the larger Germanic sphere. Regarding Odin's connection to kings and warriors, it would make more sense for his main attribute to be a sword - the weapon of choice for heroes in Norse sagas (Adams 1997: 633, Bertilsson 2017a: 65). However, Gungnir may be a residual Odinic trait connected to youthful warbands armed with spears during older periods.

A warband god does seem to have been present in early Celtic society. However, the earliest sources were written by Romans, and they named this deity Mars or Mercury due to their cultural understanding (Green 1993: 110ff). Therefore, an early name for this Celtic kóryos-god is missing. Some figures in Irish contexts, such as Lug and Finn, share many kóryos attributes. Finn was a mythical leader of the *fían* (Arbuthnot & Parsons 2012: 9). Upon entering adolescence, Finn was initiated into a *fían*, during which he received a spear, helmet, shield, and belt. As a *fían* member, he acquires the knowledge of poetry and magic, and eventually gains the leadership role of the warband (Green 1997: 98). His life is characterized by liminality and violence, and he bears similarity to Rudra and the Maruts (McCone 1986: 2, Kershaw 2000: 186). Lug, from *lugos* "raven", is a god of warriors, war magic, and crafts. He appears in several Gaulish placenames and is particularly connected to youth (Green 1997: 135, Sjoestedt 1949: 43ff). Cú Chulainn, son and reincarnation of Lug, is likewise primarily connected to warfare and occult knowledge of magic and poetry (Sjoestedt 1949: 60-9).

As Enright (1996: 276) argues, Lug and Odin share a striking number of attributes when compared. The gods are both chiefs and war leaders. Their weapons of choice are spears and war magic. Furthermore, they are patrons of poetry and the ancestors of heroes and kings. Viewed in tandem with the Indo-Iranian evidence, these characteristics strongly resemble kóryos rituals and identities. These deities may be descendants of a PIE kóryos god, associated with warbands, the elite, magic, poetry, and often spears. The similarities are especially prevalent among Celtic and Germanic kóryos groups.

#### 2.2.4. Ecstatic Fury

In several IE contexts, warriors, and whole warbands, are often described as entering a state of ecstatic fury prior to, or during, battle. Perhaps the most famous of these is the Norse *berserkir* (McCone 2012:30, 1987: 102ff). This is often achieved through ritual and magical means, sometimes granted by their patron god. Warriors who enter a possessed rage become violent and uncontrollable, often described as acting like, or literally becoming, rabid wolves (Kershaw 2000: 120ff, Schjødt 2020: 574-81). IE warriors could enter this state of uncontrolled frenzy during rituals, sometimes seen as a form of magical possession by an outside force, which was synonymously associated with esoteric magical knowledge and poetry (Lincoln 1991: 131, 1981: 130, Enright 1996: 218). Ecstatic fury thus bridges the gap between the seemingly disparate categories of warbands, magic, and poetry which frequently appear as the affinities of gods like Odin, Indra, and Lug. In some contexts, nudity seems to have been a part of these rituals. Nudity as a quasi-magical attribute of the frenzied warrior is particularly common in descriptions of Celtic and Germanic groups, which will be explored in a later section.

Homeric warriors, such as Achilles and Hector, were often described as entering a state of *lyssa* "wolf rage" in battle. A warrior could also become *mainetai* "enraged or possessed", a word describing a state of war fury specifically due to possession by a god or poetic inspiration (Cebrián 2010: 345ff, Lincoln 1991: 131). *Lyssa* is similar to when the Myrmidons act as wolves in preparation for battle in the *Iliad*. Later Greek worship of Dionysus included a type of ecstatic frenzy. This included dancing, singing, and an abandonment of societal norms. Some worshippers would act as animals by tearing apart and eating the raw flesh of cattle (Price 2019: 316). The words for this state of madness were the same as those used to describe warriors possessed with fury by Ares (Kershaw 2000: 79ff).

The Indo-Iranian word *aēšma* "fury" is similarly used to describe a state of frenzy for warriors. This state is achieved by consuming *soma*, an unidentified drug - possibly ephedra - often mentioned in the Rig Veda, which was important to warriors and poets. Additionally, by offering soma and reciting praise poetry to Indra, the god would gain *aēšma* which he, in return, grants to the offeror (Lincoln 1981: 99ff, 127, Jamison & Brereton 2014: 31). In the Gathas, Zarathustra polemically opposes "men's societies" characterized by their *aēšma* and cattle sacrifice (Eliade 1978: 305). Furthermore, *Aēšma* is another name for Rudra, father of the Marut warband (Falk 1986: 18). *Aēšma* is sometimes likened to becoming a wolf. For instance, soma consumption was said to transform men into wolves in Ancient Iran, and later Iranian Scythians were occasionally called *haumavarga* "Soma-wolves" (Kershaw 2000: 80, Lincoln 1981: 127-31).

Ecstatic fury is particularly common in Germanic contexts. As mentioned, the terms *berserkir* and *úlfhéðnar* are often used interchangeably in the Norse sagas, indicating that they may be the same phenomenon. *Berserkir* and *úlfhéðnar* may have been separate categories of warriors in the late Iron Age (Price 2019: 310), but both most likely stem from the same kóryos warband. Both appear as elite warbands of shapeshifting warriors, clad in wolf skins, that would enter a state of ecstatic fury prior to battle (Schjødt 2020: 574-83, McCone 1987: 102). This state was called *berserksgangr*, and individuals possessed by it were said to act as rabid wolves and become impervious to harm in battle (McCone 1987:102). It takes the form of an uncontrollable possession in many contexts, at times similar to werewolves of folklore. Howling occurs frequently in *berserkir* and *úlfhéðnar* stories, as well as blood-drinking (Kershaw 2000: 44, Schjødt 2020: 580, Price 2019: 304ff). It is speculated that *berserksgangr* was achieved through the ingestion of hallucinogenic mushrooms, but there is no proof of this (Adams 1997: 632ff). Weapon dancing, often with spears, may also have been a method of achieving ecstatic fury. Depictions of wolf-like warriors associated with Odin can be dated back to at least the 7th century CE in Sweden and the 5th century CE in Germany. A fresco in Kyiv painted in the 11th century CE appears to show a bare-shirted man with a wolf head armed with a spear. This motif, which appears identical to descriptions of *úlfhéðnar*, may explain why Rus' warriors would reportedly enter a furious rage and howl like animals both during and after battle (Price 2019: 306-8). The *feralis exercitus* "savage host" among the Harii in Tacitus hints at an older conception of furor-filled warriors present in Germanic contexts. They were said to spread terror by painting their bodies and shields black to resemble the dead (McCone 2012: 21ff, Kershaw 2000: 41). Furthermore, a 4th century CE Roman source describes a certain type of fury shining in the eyes of Germanic warriors in battle (McCone 2022: 183).

Odin, and the Proto-Germanic reconstruction of his name \*Wodanaz, derives from the word *wōð* or *wōd* meaning madness, possession, or poetic inspiration (Kershaw 2000: 69-72, Schjødt 2020: 1124). The Germanic root *wōb-* is semantically connected to Latin *vates* and Celtic *uatis* meaning a magically inspired "poet or seer", ultimately being rooted in the concept of ecstatic combat fury (Enright 1996: 218). Much like *aēšma*, the ecstatic fury of Norse myth is connected to both warfare and poetic inspiration. As previously mentioned, Odin's main attributes, much like Indra and Lug, are warbands and poetry. He is thus the god of both aspects of fury, and his role as a sorcerer and poet is firmly rooted in ecstasy. He acquires the mead of poetry and gains magical powers upon drinking it, and receives the knowledge of runes, in an ecstatic trance (Price 2019: 57). This bears striking resemblance to the soma-induced ecstasy of Indo-Iranian poets and warriors. Odin, like Indra, is also *Herjann* "leader of the warband". Perhaps mead, much like soma, was consumed by *berserkir* and *úlfhéðnar* to achieve a state of fury in ritual settings.

The same type of possessed fury appears in several Celtic contexts. In particular, older Roman and Greek accounts of Celtic warriors hint at the sort of ecstatic fury that may have characterized kóryos members. Celtic warfare was typically characterized as fury-induced recklessness, accompanied by war cries and drums (Faszcza 2018: 163ff, Cunliffe 2018: 228ff). For instance, Plutarch writes that the Celtiberians fought in a frenzied manner and resembled a larger raiding party than an organized army (Enright 1996: 222). For Livy, fury in battle was a typical attribute of Gauls (Faszcza 2018: 163). Gaul, and the Greek word *galatēs*, most likely comes from \*galatis, a Gaulish word meaning someone possessed by \*galā "warlike fury, valour". \*Galā is present in the Old Irish word for warrior, *láth gaile*, literally translated as "heat of fury", commonly associated with kóryos-like groups. Celtic kóryos warriors, who spread out over the Mediterranean as mercenaries between the 6th and 3rd centuries BCE, may have referred to themselves as \*galatīs "furious ones", which would explain why both Greek and Latin speakers referred to them by that native Gaulish word (McCone 2012: 23-6). Cunliffe (2018: 228ff) argues that alcohol seems to have been involved in Celtic battle fury. This is probable since drinking rituals are often connected to both Celtic and Germanic warbands (Enright 1996). As such, ingesting alcohol may have been part of the \*galatīs pre-battle rituals. The \*galā of Gauls survives in Irish myth as well. As previously mentioned, Cú Chulainn, himself part of a kóryos band, had to be dunked in water to get him out of his frenzied state prior to his reintegration into society. This fury is often associated with a fiery look in the eyes of the warrior (McCone 2002: 49, 2022: 185).

As Kershaw (2000: 16-8) argues, early IE warfare was primarily raiding-based, especially for kóryos groups. It seems that the type of combat characterized by fury fell out of use in societies that moved towards an organized military structure. For instance, *lyssa* is only mentioned once during the Classical period of Greece in reference to a Spartan who broke formation due to his frenzied fighting, which was ultimately viewed as negative at this time (Cebrián 2010: 346ff). Structural similarities between Germanic, Celtic, and older IE warfare with a focus on raids may have ideologically allowed kóryos traits to survive longer in those areas. This may explain why Celtic and Germanic groups share many traits compared to other groups. Taken together, the evidence indicates that PIE, and post-PIE, kóryos warriors induced a state of ecstatic fury in magical rituals prior to raids. These rituals could have involved heroic poetry, wolf behavior, weapon dancing, chanting, and psychoactive substances. In some cases, nudity may have played a prominent role in both fury rituals and warfare.

#### 2.2.5. Nudity and Weapon Dancing

In this section, I will present cases of nudity and weapon dancing in IE warfare and ritual contexts. I argue that both these attributes are linked to ecstatic fury and should be seen as important parts of the kóryos package. However, I have chosen to present them in a separate section since they will be especially important in later analyses as indicators of kóryos groups.

As McCone (2012: 30) argues, kóryos members seem to have expressed their identity by wearing animal skins or by foregoing clothes. Nudity holds an interesting position in IE linguistics. Words for specific body parts are relatively hard to reconstruct in PIE, barring a few examples, which indicates a possible taboo for speaking of specific body parts. However, in stark contrast, there are up to seven reconstructed words for nudity, which are often connected to magic in several contexts. Furthermore, a close semantic link between "naked" and "man" implies that nudity was seen as a particularly male attribute (Huld 1997: 75-89). The Kernosovisky idol, a large stone stele found in Ukraine dated between 4000 to 3000 BCE, may support this quasi-magical and masculine connection. The carved stele depicts a bearded male who is completely nude except for a belt, indicated by an erect phallus and nipples. A battle axe hangs on his belt, along with other weapons such as a bow and arrow. Scenes of hunting and sexual intercourse are also carved along the base of the slab (Mallory 1989: 204, Telegin & Mallory 1994: 10-4, 96). The scenes are interesting since they connect to two common kóryos attributes, life in the wild and sexual licentiousness. The "naked and belted" motif is particularly relevant due to its similarity with later descriptions and depictions of naked, sometimes ithyphallic, belted IE warriors. The belt gifted to Vedic *vrātyas* was said to represent loyalty to the warband. It symbolically tied initiates to the group and the leader (Lincoln 1981: 128). This may be the case in other IE contexts as well, although there is no mention of it. A belt also has practical functions for a nude warrior, as it can be used to fasten weapons to oneself without having to hold them, like the battle axe on the Kernosovisky idol or a sword.

Nudity seems to have been especially common in Celtic contexts, with warriors appearing naked prior to, and during, battle (Cunliffe 2018: 222). The oldest representation is a large stone statue dated to the 6th century BCE found in south Germany depicting a belted ithyphallic man wearing only a helmet and a neck torc. In his belt, a sword hangs. Similar depictions of naked and belted Gaulish warriors are common in the classical world. For instance, the Greek statue "Dying Gaul" dated to around the 3rd century BCE contains such motif, with a sword and a belt depicted under the nude Celt. An Etruscan stele from the 4th century BCE, as well as a Roman frieze from the 2nd century BCE both contrast clothed Etruscan and Roman warriors to naked and belted Gaulish warriors (McCone 2012: 27ff, 2002: 43ff). According to Polybius, the battle of Telamon in 225 BCE between Romans and Cisalpine Gauls included a separate group of *gaisatai* "spearmen" at the front of the Gaul army who fought naked. Later Irish sagas also contain fury-filled *fían* warriors appearing naked in battle (McCone 2012: 18, 27). These naked warriors are also depicted on Celtic coins (West 2007: 450). The belt is also present in Irish saga, as Finn the mythical *fían* member received a spear and a belt upon initiation (Green 1997: 98). The belt worn by Celtic warriors may represent loyalty to a kóryos-like group, like the Vedic *vrātyas*. In any case, nudity seems, as Cunliffe (2018: 222) argues, to primarily have ritualistic elements related to warfare.

Nudity among kóryos-like groups can be found in Germanic contexts as well. The earliest mention is by Tacitus, who says that Germanic warriors fight mostly in the nude (West 2007: 449). The popularly accepted etymology of *berserkir* is derived from *ber-* "bear" and *serk* "shirt", and this seems intuitively correct since warriors associated with bears are present in many Norse sagas. However, as McCone (1987: 106) argues, this etymology is fraught since the "bear" compound appears as *bjarn-* in Old Norse, while *ber-* as in "bare-shirt" makes more sense morphologically. Furthermore, *berserkir* are often synonymously equated with *úlfhéðnar* "wolf skins" but never as *bjarnhéðnar* "bear skins" or vice versa. Wearing wolf skins and being naked are not necessarily contradictory. Since *héðinn* denotes a cloak of fur covering the head and upper torso like a hood (Kershaw 2000: 157), one could be an *úlfhéðinn* and simultaneously be naked or bare-shirted underneath. The 11th century CE Kievan Rus' fresco depicting a bare-shirted, wolf-headed, and spear-wielding man mentioned earlier supports this etymology. An older connection can also be made, as both Tacitus and Julius Caesar mention naked or lightly clad warriors armed with spears in connection with Germanic groups (McCone 1986: 19). Furthermore, the naked and belted motif also appears in a 7th century CE buckle found in East England. Dubbed the "Finglesham Man", it depicts a nude man in a dance-like pose, only wearing a belt and a horned helmet, with two spears in each hand (Ringquist 1969: 294, Kershaw 2000: 90). In other IE contexts, the martial association with nudity is scant. The exception is in book 12 of the *Iliad*, where the Lykians appear to be fighting naked or bare-shirted, as the weapons are said to be able to pierce their "naked backs" when they turn around. Furthermore, the later Greek ephebes were lightly clad during their military training (Cebrián 2010: 348–52). The ritual aspect of nudity remained in other contexts. For instance, the *lupreci* of Rome were naked during the Lupercalia festival (Vukovic 2018: 46). Athletes in the Greek Olympic games were all male and competed in the nude, and nude shaman-like figures frequently appear in the Rig Veda as well (Huld 1997: 89ff).

Another important attribute of the kóryos package is weapon dancing. Weapon dancing appears to have ritual significance in the contexts it occurs. It is frequently associated with martial situations, often in groups and before combat. As Kershaw (2000: 83) argues, weapon dancing was most likely a method of achieving ecstatic fury. This type of dance is associated with kóryos members themselves, but also their patron gods.

In Celtic contexts, Livy mentions a war dance that preceded combat among Celts (Cunliffe 2018: 227). The magical aspect of this may be seen in the later Irish story of Lug who performs a type of ritual dance and song in which he closes one eye and jumps on one leg while chanting incantations prior to battle (McCone 2022: 188–91). In the Rig Veda, both Indra and the Maruts frequently appear as dancers (Falk 1986: 65, Kershaw 2000: 100). The *Salii*, from Latin *salire* "dancing", would perform ritual dances during the Quinquatrus festival in March. The dance may have marked the beginning of the war season (Bailey & North 2005). The Greek Kouretes or Korybantes also consisted of young male weapon dancers with a ritual, and specifically ecstatic, association. They were compared to the *Salii* by contemporary Graeco-Roman writers. Furthermore, the Greek historian Xenophon writes in the 4th century BCE of a ritual war dance he witnessed performed by Thracians, Arcadians, and other groups in Anatolia. The dance also included mock battles and chanting (Kershaw 2000: 86–98).

Weapon dancing appears most frequently in Germanic contexts. Tacitus writes of a weapon dance among Germanic groups that involved jumping between spears and swords. This dance was performed by naked youths and was most likely part of a ritual (Schjødt 2020: 571). The weapon dancing motif is commonly depicted in material sources throughout the Iron Age (Holmqvist 1961). Among them, the Torslunda plates (Fig. 1), dated to the 7th century CE, most clearly connect weapon dancing to *úlfhéðnar* and Odin. The plate, intended to be a helmet plaque, depicts two armed figures. One of the figures, assumed to be Odin, is in a dance-like pose with bended knees and raised arms, in which two spears are held. The figure is naked except for a helmet and a belt, bearing a strong resemblance to the Finglesham Man. The other figure is clad in a wolf skin and mask covering the head and torso. The figure is also holding a spear and appears to be in the motion of unsheathing a sword (Price 2019: 308). Similar finds depicting Odinic-like figures and wolf-warriors armed with spears in dance-like poses appear several times throughout Northern Europe between the 5th and the 11th century CE, clearly linked by a common tradition (Price 2019: 320-3, Ringquist 1969: 292-5). A 10th-century CE Byzantine eye-witness account of a Christmas ceremony performed by the Varangian guard - warriors from Northern Europe - sheds further light on the weapon dance. During this procession, Varangians, clad in animal skins and armed with staffs and shields, perform a choreographed war dance during which they make noise by bashing their staffs and shield together while chanting "jul" (Price 2019: 306ff). The attire of these dancers hints that they may be *úlfhéðnar*, and though it is performed during a celebration, the dance has martial and ritual undertones. Furthermore, the Yule association connects this dance with the winter solstice, the time at which kóryos rituals appear to often take place.

### 2.3. Reintegration

After the liminal kóryos period was over, members would be reintegrated into society as adults with rights to marry and own property (McCone 1987: 140-2). The ritual associated with reintegration is, just as the initial initiation, difficult to characterize. In some contexts, members had to pass a martial test. For instance, Tacitus describes the young warriors of the Chatti and Harii as having an unkept and fierce appearance. They let their beard and hair grow and they wear an iron ring. They can only shave and remove the ring once they have killed an enemy in battle (Schjødt 2020: 572ff). In a Norse context, this may have involved challenging and fighting an older warrior (Kershaw 2000: 58). Reintegration also often takes the form of ritual death and rebirth (Harris 2008: 304). For instance, Sinfjötli's death and resurrection by Sigmundr at the end of their wolfish episode is connected to their reintegration into society (Schjødt 2008: 308). In Celtic contexts, this rebirth was achieved by members being submerged in a water-filled cauldron, as seen by Cú Chulainn's return to his village and depictions on the Gundestrup cauldron (McCone 1987: 112ff). Since the ecstatic fury that characterized these fighters was seen as a physical heat radiating off the body, a water submersion metaphorically cooled off the members' fury prior to reintegration.

Upon reintegration, members would most likely fill positions of power, such as chiefs or as part of elite warbands, due to their elite backgrounds. This is the case in several mythological stories and accounts of warbands previously mentioned. As such, kóryos groups share similar exclusionary attributes with secret societies, and they may be characterized as "schools" for the elite. However, some members seem to continually identify and live as kóryos warriors into adulthood. They formed special warbands distinguished from other members of society. For instance, some Chatti retained their unkept appearance and iron ring continually (Schjødt 2020: 572). *Berserkir* and *úlfhéðnar* may follow a similar division, wherein the latter represented youths and the *berserkir* were older warriors who never left their liminal identity as Odinic shapeshifting warriors (Kershaw 2000: 61, Schjødt 2020: 583). In Irish saga, Finn, unlike Cú Chulainn, never leaves his liminal *fían* existence despite reaching adulthood (McCone 1986: 2). In Vedic contexts, some *vrātyas* would be unable to enter normal society due to a lack of property, despite their elite backgrounds (Kershaw 2000: 130). These older kóryos warriors may have served as the instructors for initiates, or as mercenaries in different retinues.

### 2.4. The Ideology of Kóryos

The widespread occurrence of kóryos-like groups throughout the IE-speaking world attests to the success of the kóryos ideology. The ideology and the group changed and adapted due to historical trajectories, but structural traces of the original PIE warband remained, especially in raiding-based IE societies.

The purpose of kóryos appears to have been two-fold. The first is to form individuals with fighting capabilities at the command of one or several chiefs, which can provide socioeconomic gain in the form of loot, intimidation, and territorial expansion. For instance, Germanic kóryos-like groups were, in addition to raids, used to enforce disputes within society (Enright 1996: 203). Furthermore, kóryos members may have partaken in executions (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a: 190). This reflects a group that could be mobilized for violent and coercive means. Secondly, kóryos appears to have an educational purpose, reflected by the young age of the members and the significance placed on heroic poetry. The poetry and myths served as ideological constructions aimed at schooling the elite. For example, the Cattle Raid myth, represented in several IE contexts, tells the story of an IE warrior who, after having his cattle stolen by a monster or an outsider, leaves his society to slay the perpetrator and rightfully take back his cattle by force (Lincoln 1976, Walcot 1979, West 2007: 451ff). As Lincoln (1991: 11ff) argues, the myth heroizes raiding by casting it in the light of a ritually sanctioned and morally justified action. Cattle in this case could be substituted for any other commodity, such as furs or slaves. This schooling was for the elite. As such, it should not be likened to a puberty ritual where all boys in society are included. Much like a secret society, kóryos was inherently exclusionary as it only fostered social cohesion among elite males.

Another important aspect of kóryos is its members' identification with wolves. The links between wolves, death, and outlaws may underlie the warband's connection to the animal. As warriors engaged in raiding, sexual licentiousness, and other taboo behavior, a wolf would be a fitting power animal within this particular cosmology. The ideological move of identifying as a wolf may create social cohesion within the warband, as they raid and kill in a pack. However, it also allowed its members to dehumanize themselves through identification with the bestial, criminal, and taboo aspects of wolves. It allowed members to temporarily abandon human attributes such as compassion and fear by ritually becoming predatory animals hunting their prey. Ultimately, this promoted a willingness to kill and served as an ideological justification for it (Lincoln 1991: 145, Price 2019: 315-19). Nudity may be related to this animalistic identity because it implies the rejection of human clothing (West 2007: 450). Most likely, however, nudity was used to accentuate the masculine and libidinal qualities of ecstatic fury, which was imbued with magico-ritual properties in martial settings. Wolfishness, ecstatic fury, and by extension, nudity, were different aspects of a socially sanctioned auto-dehumanization by IE warriors.

Kóryos members may not have thought of themselves as actual wolves. Non-members may neither have thought of them as such but were simply coerced enough to accept it. Either way, ideology lay in practices rather than beliefs, and as long as they acted as wolves, it was permissible to engage in violent and taboo behavior. This permissibility may have been compounded by the members' elite backgrounds. Modern psychological studies indicate that individuals with higher social power tend to be less compassionate and suffer less distress at witnessing others' suffering compared to those with less power (van Kleef et al. 2008, Stellar et al. 2012). In addition, wealthy individuals tend to be more likely to view themselves as above morals and engage in unethical behavior, such as stealing (Hayden 2018: 17). Traits like these are continually fostered within kóryos groups through positive validation in myth, ritual, and raids.

To conclude, kóryos can be viewed as a form of warrior education for future members of the upper social stratum in PIE and post-PIE groups. The institution fostered social cohesion among the elite, provided raiding and warfare capabilities, and schooled its members in rituals and myths that provided ideological justifications for violent and exclusionary attitudes. A wolf persona that allowed members to engage in taboo and violent behavior, paired with rituals and myths intended to boost martial prowess through dehumanization, could most likely produce attributes similar to sociopathy among the elite. In short, the kóryos ideology was a highly effective way to reproduce the conditions for a stratified raiding-based society.

## 3. Kóryos and the Nordic Bronze Age

In this chapter, I argue for the presence of historically and culturally contingent kóryos groups specific to the NBA based on a two-folded approach. First, an overview and discussion of NBA society is presented. Although not direct proof of kóryos, this part provides a historical context focused on socioeconomic structures related to warfare, trade, and ritual. Specifically, the purpose is to both contextualize later interpretations and highlight structural similarities between NBA retinues and kóryos-like groups. This first section is, in part, necessary to avoid overdetermining rock art's role in my interpretations. Furthermore, contacts between Paleo-Celtic and Paleo-Germanic groups in the NBA are proposed to explain why later Celtic and Germanic kóryos-like groups share many similarities compared to other IE contexts.

The second approach focuses on rock art motifs and how they correspond to kóryos attributes explored in the previous chapter. Typological similarities between rock art and kóryos attributes attest to the appearance of kóryos groups in the NBA and, at the same time, are used to characterize the praxis and rituals of NBA kóryos groups. Kóryos may, as I argue, have played an important role in NBA society by educating the elite and providing warriors.

### 3.1. Society and Warriorhood in the NBA

#### 3.1.1. Maritime Mode of Production (MMP)

In this section, I will discuss the social and material relations of production in the NBA and the role warriors played within them, typified by Ling et al. (2018b) as the Maritime Mode of Production (MMP). This section is meant to contextualize the praxis of possible secret societies and kóryos groups and explore their raison d'être within the NBA.

Vandkilde (2016) argues that the BA can be defined as a form of pre-modern globalization, a process she calls "Bronzization". Between 2000 and 1200 BCE, several regions of Europe, North Africa, and Asia were connected through the trade of bronze, which led to a previously unprecedented level of connectivity characterized by vast trade networks (Vandkilde 2016). This is partially because copper and tin, the metals needed for bronze production, could only be sourced from a few disparate places (Kristiansen & Earle 2015: 241). A characteristic of this bronzization was, as Kristiansen (2018a: 87) defines it, "a regional, globalized, division of labor for the first time in world history". With the rise of productive centers such as Mesopotamia and Egypt came the rise of the Bronze Age peripheries, and economic structures of center-periphery exchange. Products such as amber, wool, salt, and slaves were traded from the periphery to the center in return for prestige items such as bronze, gold, and silver (Kristiansen 2018a: 87-91). Long-distance trade networks and confederacies were established transregionally, and a flow of trade consisting of both commodity exchange and prestige goods flourished (Kristiansen 2023: 241-4). However, goods moving to periphery regions, such as Scandinavia, were largely metals and items of prestige limited mostly to the elite (Hayden & Earle 2022: 390).

The political process that channeled vast amounts of metals and other goods to groups in southern Scandinavia during the NBA is referred to by Ling et al. (2018b) as the Maritime Mode of Production. Modes of production are difficult to define. However, as Kristiansen & Earle (2022: 133) argue, they are models used to "describe how surplus labour and surplus wealth [is] generated and distributed". This entails not only goods and their production but also the institutions that control or support them, such as traders and warriors. The MMP is argued to characterize both the NBA and the later Viking Age in Scandinavia, emphasizing the maritime aspects of both societies and the role warriors played within them. The MMP is typified as a system of decentralized elite groups, consolidating power through gift exchanges and the financing of warbands that protected trade routes and brought in further wealth through raids. Elites used agro-pastoral surpluses to finance boats and raids to gain wealth and political power through maritime ventures. Both the NBA and the Viking Age shared a similar political model based upon decentralized complexity, seaborne warriors, and the raiding and trading of metals, prestige items, and slaves (Ling et al. 2018b, Kristiansen & Earle 2022: 136). Similar to Viking and Celtic warfare of the Iron Age, maritime NBA warbands protected traveling traders, secured trade routes, and engaged in raids (Kristiansen 2016, Kristiansen & Suchowska-Ducke 2015: 385). Large technological and sociological differences between these periods should not be ignored. However, there are deep historical structures present in the NBA and the Viking Age leading to similar political and societal structures (Ling et al. 2018b: 488, Kristiansen 2016: 184).

The importance of the MMP for this analysis is the key role warriors played within it. The Bronze Age saw the emergence of militarized societies throughout Eurasia, described by Horn & Kristiansen (2018: 1) as "warrior aristocracies", with a new class of warriors, institutionalized warfare, and new forms of weaponry. Weapon types such as swords, shields, and cuirasses were either invented or became widespread during this period (Molloy & Horn 2020: 117). In the NBA, the seaborne and mobile warrior served as one of the most important figures in upholding the MMP, both ideologically and by force. Much like kóryos, NBA warriorhood contained several ritualized elements, as shown by rock art and votive weapon deposits in wetlands (Horn & Kristiansen 2018: 1-4).

Warfare in the BA was not a peaceful ritual act, and weapons were not purely symbolic (c.f Johansen 2000, Treherne 1995). Analyses of skeletal trauma as well as wear on weaponry point to violence as a reality for most BA people. Both large-scale warfare, massacres of entire villages, and skeleton trauma from weaponry are present in the archaeological record of northern Europe during this time (Lidke et al. 2018, Harding et al. 2007, Fyllingen 2006, Tornberg 2022). Although warfare was never endemic, it was nonetheless widespread and formed a large presence within society (Vandkilde 2013a: 54ff, 2015, 2018:233). Not surprisingly, warriors stood as the backbone of warfare, and they could be seen as the product of two interlinked institutions: the ideologically constructed warrior identity, and the organization of warriors into semi-professional warbands, both of which emerged during this time (Horn & Kristiansen 2018: 4, Molloy & Horn 2020). In particular, the identity and organization of BA and NBA warbands may have roots in earlier Neolithic kóryos groups, which became institutionalized during this period (Kristiansen 2022: 66).

Although not found in Scandinavia, a kóryos-like group may be identifiable in a Bronze Age cemetery in Neckarsulm, Germany. This gravesite, dated to 1200 BCE, consists of around 50 individuals, all males, and of varying ages, however, most are young males. Several of them were buried with typical warrior goods, such as swords, razors, and metal rings. The men were well-fed and shared the same healthy diet, with about half of them taller than the average height in that region and period, indicating that the men were of high social status. Signs of skeletal wear show that these men were physically active and relatively muscular, and many showed skeletal changes associated with horseback riding. Furthermore, one-third of all individuals were of non-local origin (Wahl & Price 2013). Wahl & Price (2013: 304) and Kristiansen (2018a: 24) conclude that this represents a male society, or warband, consisting of both local and non-local warriors. Traveling bands of warriors, like the ones buried at Neckarsulm, served the purpose of protecting trade expeditions and raiding for wealth, but they could also serve as mercenaries in foreign armies, bringing home prestige items and knowledge (Horn & Kristiansen 2018: 4, Kristiansen 2018b: 26ff). Individuals in these traveling warbands may have been different kóryos members or groups joining together or joining larger groups during their raiding season. This could include both young kóryos members and senior warriors who continuously inhabit their liminal identity. The extensive occurrence of flange-hilted swords and uniform warrior burials throughout Europe between 1500 and 1100 BCE indicate that both warriors and ideas of warriorship travelled vast distances (Kristiansen 2018b: 24). As mentioned, kóryos groups seem to be able to combine and subsequently divide depending on the undertaking. This may have occurred over large regions during the NBA, as different kóryos groups seasonally formed transregional warbands that engaged in raiding or mercenary work.

Warriorship in the NBA was not a transient or temporary role taken on by individuals in situations where violence was deemed necessary. It was a social identity, defined by warfare and weapon handling, as well as an association with other warriors. While warriors participated in other activities at home, the identification with warfare defined their identity. This is in large part because access to warriorship was regulated institutionally within society by warrior fraternities (Vandkilde 2018: 230). The institutionalization of warriorship was driven by elites' consolidation of power through, for example, control over access to metals (Kristiansen 2022: 19). However, the maritime aspect may also have contributed. A seaborne warrior would have to possess specific skills related to the maritime sphere, such as ship navigation or weather conditions. Furthermore, if they also functioned as traders, or at least accompanied long-distance trade ventures, knowledge of trade relations and foreign customs would be necessary (Ling & Cornell 2017: 29ff). An NBA-specific kóryos may have involved an education in maritime skills in addition to warfare and ritual. This would ensure that the knowledge needed for maritime ventures was continually regulated and reproduced within the elite. These elite groups must have had a vested interest in continually reproducing educational institutions to secure a continued flow of metals in the future. Kóryos may have filled this function. Although not direct proof of kóryos, the MMP highlights the central role warriors played in the NBA, and the institutionalized warbands of the period share structural similarities with later kóryos groups. This institutionalization may have ideologically sustained and continually reproduced kóryos groups in the NBA.

#### 3.1.2. The Celto-Germanic Connection

In Chapter 2, I briefly touched upon some noticeable similarities between Germanic and Celtic kóryos groups. This includes a focus on nudity in ritual and warfare, with naked troops appearing alongside clothed warriors among both groups. Specifically, the naked and belted motif appears more frequently compared to other IE contexts. Furthermore, a strong connection between kóryos and spears in ritual and myth is pronounced among Germanic and Celtic groups, evidenced by Odin and Lug.

Enright (1996: 285) argues that similarities between Germanic and Celtic warbands are due to contacts occurring around the beginning of the 1st millennium CE. However, this is an *argumentum ex silentio*. Since no textual sources describe Germanic groups before this period, one cannot definitively say that similarities did not arise at an earlier date (Schjødt 2020: 561ff). In fact, there is archaeolinguistic evidence that points to a Germanic and Celtic connection present in the NBA sustained by long-distance travel and contact (Koch & Ling 2023). This connection may push these shared traits further back in time, and ultimately explain the Celto-Germanic (CG) kóryos similarities as stemming from the NBA.

As previously mentioned, NBA societies were peripherally connected by trade and long-distance contact with several regions outside of Scandinavia. Metal was imported to Scandinavia in exchange for Baltic amber and, perhaps, slaves (Chacon et al. 2020: 89). Chemical analyses of bronze items found in Scandinavia indicate that metals were not locally mined. Rather, they were mostly imported from Central Europe, the British Isles, and the Iberian Peninsula (Ling et al. 2013, Ling et al. 2019). Furthermore, strong similarities between rock art found in Iberia and Scandinavia point to a shared warriorship tradition present in both regions during the NBA (Díaz-Guardamino et al. 2022).

The regions of North and West Europe have historically been inhabited by Germanic and Celtic-speaking groups respectively, and genetic studies indicate that no large migration into these regions took place between the onset of the BA (around 2000–1700 BCE) and historical times. This indicates that Germanic and Celtic languages most likely developed within the same regions they were historically spoken, and that a form of Paleo-Germanic and Paleo-Celtic was spoken there during the BA and NBA, with Paleo-Germanic arriving to, and spreading in, Scandinavia during the Late Neolithic (Koch 2018: 1-6, McColl et al. 2024). A specific CG vocabulary is shared between Germanic and Celtic languages, but not with other IE branches, that can linguistically be dated to have occurred before 500 BCE but after 2000 BCE - chronologically very close to the NBA. This linguistic CG connection is largely due to cultural contacts rather than just the branches' shared ancestry. The CG corpus contains many words related to warfare, the maritime sphere, stratified social roles, and secret knowledge (Koch 2020: 79ff, Koch 2018).

Taken together, the archaeological and linguistic evidence points to sustained contact between Paleo-Germanic speaking groups in North Europe and Paleo-Celtic speaking groups in West Europe during the NBA. Rock art motifs and the CG vocabulary strongly support the hypothesis that the agents of this contact were elite groups of maritime traders and raiding warriors (Koch & Ling 2023). Interestingly, many CG words directly correspond to kóryos attributes explored in the previous chapter. For instance, the words for werewolf, ecstatic fury, male youth, oath, poetry, magic, and spears are all part of the CG vocabulary (Koch 2018: 9-14). Furthermore, the Welsh word *gweilgi* "sea, ocean" is a compound of the words for wolf and dog, meaning that the word for "wolf" was used as a metaphor for "sea" in earlier periods (Stifter 2023: 178). This points to a shared maritime kóryos tradition among Paleo-Germanic and Paleo-Celtic groups during the NBA.

Ling & Uhnér (2014: 38) argue that the elite group of traders and warriors that participated in long-distance exchange during the NBA likely had more in common with elites from other distant regions than with local groups due to intensive contact. Since kóryos members were typically part of the elite, they may have taken part in these long-distance travels, perhaps to protect traders or as a form of education for members. This sustained contact may have mutually influenced kóryos praxis and ritual among both groups. In social network theory, "homophily" is used to describe how similar groups or people are more likely to be in contact with each other, which acts as a feedback loop that continually reinforces similar ideas and attitudes (Cavazzuti et al. 2022: 46). As Paleo-Germanic and Paleo-Celtic groups met during long-distance sea voyages, certain ideas about kóryos may have been continually facilitated by these contacts. This may explain the increased ritual focus on nudity and spears among Celto-Germanic kóryos groups compared to other IE contexts.

### 3.2. Rock Art and Kóryos

This section will be used to compare common motifs found in rock art with the kóryos attributes of Chapter 2. This will be used to both attest to the existence of kóryos in the NBA and to hypothesize the praxis, rituals, and ideology of NBA-specific kóryos groups. Some attributes are particularly frequent, such as spears, nudity, and weapon dancing. Others, like a connection to wolves, are noticeably absent in NBA rock art, which will be discussed at the end of the chapter. These depictions should be seen as ideological representations of kóryos practices and rituals, and magical devices carved to magically bring about a desired result. Accentuated features are thus ideologically and magically motivated, rather than simply based on stylistic choice.

#### 3.2.1. Spearmen and Weapon Dancers

A common motif in NBA rock art is anthropomorphic figures armed with weapons, such as spears, swords, axes, bows, and shields (Skoglund et al. 2022: 181). As Horn (2022) argues, depictions of armed figures appear gendered due to a stronger association between male figures and weaponry than with female figures and weaponry. Rock art in general is largely skewed toward male depictions, indicating a preoccupation with constructions of masculinity (Horn 2017). This is even more evident in depictions of armed figures and the construction of warrior identity. Horn (2022: 6ff) shows that out of 3742 anthropomorphic figures in Swedish rock art, around 977 of them can be identified as male due to their genitalia. Only 32 figures can be identified as female primarily through depictions of breasts or birthing scenes. The rest, 2733 figures, are sexless. Out of all male figures, 74% are depicted with weaponry. This includes weapons held in their hands and sheathed swords. On the other hand, only 34% of sexless figures and 6% of female figures are depicted with weaponry. This reflects a male-gendered warrior identity during the NBA (Horn 2022: 6ff). Although female warriors cannot be ruled out, constructions of warriorhood through rock art seem to largely equate maleness, weaponry, and violence. The phalluses especially point to a close connection between male nudity and warriorhood, which will be explored in the next section. Kóryos members were always described as males and rock art largely reflects a focus on male warriors, indicating a possible correspondence. Below I argue that depictions of spears and figures with raised arms and bended knees may point to kóryos members engaged in weapon dancing.

A spear, sometimes along with a belt, shield, and helmet, was frequently given to kóryos initiates in several IE contexts. Spears are also associated with the different patron gods of kóryos, like Odin and Lug. While heroes in IE literature, especially Norse sagas, mostly wield swords, kóryos-like groups wield spears - a dichotomy that seems to be largely mirrored in NBA contexts as well (Bertilsson 2017a: 64ff). Spears made of bronze appear as a common weapon during the NBA (Horn 2018). Use-wear analyses indicate that both swords and spears were frequently used in combat in the same close-range style. Spears, much like swords, were resharpened several times, indicating frequent use (Horn 2015: 210, Kristiansen 2002). Based on grave goods, Felding et al. (2020: 13ff) argue that three separate warrior identities can be identified in the NBA through the combination of deposited weapons. The three being swords alone, swords and spears or axes together, or spears and axes alone. This may represent different ranks within a warband, with a sword-wielding leader and a retinue of spear-wielding members (Kristiansen 2018b: 28). Indeed, swords are argued to have been symbols of social status and power by chiefs and freemen (Bunnefeld 2018). However, swords may also have served as identity markers for several kinds of warriors, which seems especially prevalent in rock art (Felding et al. 2020: 14). These separate identities may be indicative of kóryos groups, consisting of initiates with spears and a sword-wielding leader or instructor.

In rock art, the distinction in rank based on weaponry is not as clear. Most armed figures in rock art are depicted with swords, but they are almost always in a sheath hanging passively by the waist (Horn 2022: 6ff, 2019: 55). Spears, however, are depicted in "active" positions, either raised overhead, held vertically, or held horizontally in lowered arms. However, the latter two of these appear much less frequently than the overhead position (Horn 2019: 45, Skoglund et al. 2022: 185-8). The active position of spears and axes in rock art has traditionally been interpreted as scenes of battles or duals. For instance, Vandkilde (2013b: 172, 2013a: 51) interprets large scenes consisting of several armed humanoids as complex depictions of fighting scenes and narrative warrior epics, like the *Iliad*. However, the weapons rarely touch the "opponent" or harm them, except for a few examples (Nordbladh 1989: 327, Ling & Toreld 2018: 68-71). Rather, as Maddox (2020) argues, depictions of naked armed figures in active positions may depict weapon dancing rituals.

**[Figure 2. A panel in Tanum depicting a possible group of weapon dancers. Author: Asa Fredell (2008) ©shfa.dh.gu.se.]**

As argued in Chapter 2, the weapon dancing motif occurs frequently in late Iron Age contexts in Scandinavia, Germany, and England. This motif typically depicts figures armed with weapons in poses that indicate active motion, specifically dancing. The figures are typically naked or lightly clothed, with a belt around the waist being the most discernible visual feature of their attire (Maddox 2020: 4). The active motion is conveyed by a slight bend in the knee along with active arms bent at the elbow raised to the sides. Nudity is most clearly conveyed by the phallic Finglesham Man. However, other figures such as the one on the Torslunda plate have no discernible clothing except for two belts and a helmet. Furthermore, the dancers are most frequently wielding one or several spears (Price 2019: 308, 323). Several rock art scenes depict similar motifs and characteristics. For instance, a panel in Tanum (Fig. 2) depicts a group of humanoid figures in similar poses. The figures are armed with spears that are raised in active positions overhead. The spears are typically held in either one or both hands but despite this, both arms are raised overhead. Some scenes depict similar figures with only one arm raised overhead. However, these are not as common. The figures also have a slight bend in the knee indicated by their protruding kneecaps which, similar to later motifs, could indicate an active dancing motion (Maddox 2020: 15).

Perhaps the most famous figure depicted in a similar motif is the 238 cm tall so-called "Spear God" in Litsleby, commonly interpreted as an Odinic-like deity (Bertilsson 2017a: 66). This figure likewise has a slight bend in the knee, raised arms, and a spear in one hand. While this figure may depict something similar to a kóryos patron god, such as Odin, it may also depict a kóryos member engaged in a weapon dance. 3D analyses of the carving indicate that the spear was carved at an earlier date than the figure, which was added at a later period (Bertilsson 2017a: 71). The spear may have had ritual significance on its own in earlier periods but was reconstituted into the weapon dance motif later to suit specific ritual needs.

**[Figure 3. A panel in Boglösa depicting the so-called "adorant" motif, which may be a weapon dancer. Author: Einar Kjellén (1975) ©shfa.dh.gu.se.]**

Some figures are depicted in the same poses but without a weapon in hand. These appear both alone and in groups, either with similar figures or among figures wielding weapons. These figures have been dubbed "adorants" and they are typically interpreted as raising their arms in a worshipping pose (Fuglestvedt 1999, Ling 2008: 204). However, many of these appear equipped with sheathed swords in martial settings. They often mirror the same pose as weapon dancers, with raised arms, a slight bend in the knee, and an erect phallus but without a weapon in hand (Fig. 3). They are also frequently depicted in groups of weapon dancers bearing no discernible difference between them except for the lack of weaponry in their hands. Because of this, I argue that the adorant motif may similarly depict kóryos members engaged in weapon dancing. The term "weapon dancing" may be misleading in this case since no weapon is wielded. However, the sheathed sword hanging by their waist indicates that they are warriors, and the pose appears strikingly similar to the other weapon dancing motifs. These rock art scenes may depict figures performing a weapon dance without weapons in hand, either alone or in tandem with other armed weapon dancers. As argued in Chapter 2, the dance performed by kóryos groups may have been a ritual method to achieve states of ecstatic fury before raids. Accentuating the dance rather than the weapon may have been more important in a magical and ritual sense if the intention was to first and foremost cause ecstatic fury. Viewed through this lens, the motif of armed and unarmed warriors with bent knees and raised arms may depict kóryos members engaged in weapon dancing.

#### 3.2.2. Naked and Belted

Nudity is another important attribute of kóryos in general and weapon dancing in particular. In some IE contexts, nudity appears as a warrior attribute with ritual and magic connections, especially in Celtic and Germanic groups. The foregoing of human clothing may have been part of the animalistic identity of kóryos members, or it may have served as psychological warfare (West 2007: 450, Kershaw 2000: 41). However, the specific contexts in which nudity appears - weapon dances and warfare - point rather to a magico-ritual connection among warriors.

Nudity is a particularly common attribute in NBA rock art. Out of all human-like figures found in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark dated to the NBA, 25% of them display male genitalia, and almost all of them are erect (Horn 2017: 245). Phalluses in rock art have traditionally been interpreted as symbols of fertility or as parts of symbolic duals in agricultural rites (Almgren 1927). However, the violent reality of the BA and the association between nudity and violence in several IE contexts make this interpretation less likely. Rather, the commonly depicted naked and armed warrior figures indicate a close connection between ideas of male nudity and violence in NBA rock art (Horn 2013: 238ff). The hyper-masculinity of rock art, a term used by Bevan (2015) to describe motifs containing exaggerated phalluses, weapons, and warriors, could be indicative of kóryos practice and ideology. A social reality may be glimpsed in these motifs, one that equates nudity and virility to the construction of warriorship (Horn 2013: 239ff). This is similar to later Celtic and Germanic contexts where warriors appear nude.

Phalluses are particularly pronounced and thus easy to identify on rock art figures, but the naked and belted motif is not as clearly depicted. Nevertheless, there is reason to suspect that the figures are naked and belted. Features that do not impact the outline of the figure, such as a belt or nipples, are difficult to identify due to the two-dimensional and silhouetted aspect of rock art images. However, a sheathed sword at the waist does imply a belt for it to hang on, while a phallus implies that the figure is not wearing clothes. These common figures could therefore be naked and belted. The clearest depiction of the naked and belted motif comes from a panel in Kville (Fig. 4). In this panel, a group of phallic humanoid figures equipped with spears and sheathed swords are depicted in the weapon dance pose described earlier. The figures have a distinct elongated uncarved shape around the waist area, which bears a striking resemblance to a belt. In the figure on the left, the shape is connected to the uncarved area between the legs. However, the shape on the right figure is distinctly on the body around the waist area, with the body continuing underneath, indicating a belt. This panel is perhaps the clearest depiction of the naked and belted motif in NBA rock art.

**[Figure 4. A panel in Kville depicting two naked and belted figures. Author: Torsten Högberg ©shfa.dh.gu.se.]**

Other similar figures may depict this motif as well. Sheathed swords may have acted as an identity marker for warriorhood in NBA rock art. This is not to say that swords were simply symbolic. Use-wear analyses of swords from the NBA indicate that they were frequently used (Kristiansen 2002). However, this is not reflected in rock art since spears and axes are the weapons depicted as actively wielded. A sheathed sword hanging by the waist may have been a way for rock art carvers to stylistically depict a belt. This would identify the figure as naked and belted while simultaneously accentuating its connection to warriorhood through identification with the sword. A naked figure with a sheathed sword may not have been intended to indirectly depict a kóryos member's belt. However, a sheathed sword hanging by the waist and a phallus implies that the depicted figures are naked and belted. Furthermore, the figures on the Kville panel clearly demonstrate that the naked and belted motif existed in NBA rock art. This may indicate that similar figures are also naked and belted.

As mentioned in Chapter 2, the oldest depiction of this motif is the Kernosovisky idol dated to the 3rd millennium BCE. In the Rig Veda, the belt is said to symbolize loyalty to the warband and members were given one upon initiation into the group. Similarly, belts were given to initiates in Celtic contexts, and belts are characteristic of naked weapon dancers in Germanic contexts. The belt may likewise have represented kóryos members' devotion to the group and its leader in the NBA as well. Belts are also practical for naked warriors as it allows them to carry equipment, such as a sword, without needing to hold it. Nudity is particularly common in Celto-Germanic contexts, most often as quasi-magical warrior attributes. This is most likely the case in NBA contexts as well. The commonly depicted ithyphallic figures with sheathed swords may depict naked and belted kóryos members. At times, they are depicted engaged in weapon dances, and these two attributes often appear together. The hyper-masculinity of rock art, evidenced by the frequent coupling of phalluses and weaponry, corresponds to the close semantic link between "man" and "naked" in PIE. Both hint at a constructed phallic warrior identity in rock art and IE thought. However, the erect, pronounced, and exaggerated proportions of phalluses in NBA rock art indicate that they represented more than just nudity. Rather, the phallic imagery might be connected to ecstatic fury as an ideological and magical accentuation of fighting prowess.

#### 3.2.3. Boat Crews

The pervading maritime focus of NBA society, in both praxis and ideology, indicates that NBA-specific kóryos groups would likely be maritime as well. As such, rock art motifs of boats with crews may depict kóryos groups setting off on, or returning from, sea-borne raids and expeditions. Attributes in rock art, such as the crew size of rock art boats, correspond closely to the size of kóryos groups in several IE contexts, which may support this maritime interpretation. Furthermore, depictions of enlarged and horned warriors together with similar, but smaller, warriors - a motif that occurs in both maritime and non-maritime settings - may point to the occurrence of senior leaders and instructors of kóryos groups, which likewise occurs in some IE contexts.

Boats in rock art are not simply symbolic images with no relation to maritime praxis. Rather, rock art boats most likely represent idealized depictions of actual plank-built warboats present in the NBA (Ling 2012). Unfortunately, no boat dated to the NBA has been found in Scandinavia. However, the Hjortspring boat, found on the Danish island of Als and dated to 340 BCE, stylistically and proportionally matches boat depictions in rock art. Although this archaeological find is from the PRIA and thus chronologically later than the NBA, the similarities do bear witness to a long boat-building tradition stretching back to at least the beginning of the NBA (Ling 2008: 187-91, Ling et al. 2018a: 152). Boat motifs may thus be used to infer the praxis and organization of maritime ventures in this period.

In rock art from Bohuslän, most depicted boats contain a crew size of around six to 13 individuals. This is largely mirrored in Uppland as well, with crew sizes of between six to twelve individuals. Furthermore, ship settings on Gotland depict boats with similar crew sizes of under 20 individuals. Larger rock art ships containing up to 135 individuals occur as well, though they are not as common. They may represent larger warships (Ling 2012: 480ff, 2008: 186).

Interestingly, these boat crew sizes are similar to the size of many kóryos groups. As discussed in the previous chapter, kóryos members often appear in groups of five to twelve in several IE contexts. The latter number is especially common and is, for instance, reflected in the typical size of a *berserkir* group (Kershaw 2000: 126ff). Kóryos groups seem to merge and divide depending on the activity and larger groups of 50 to 150 appear as well, though not as commonly. Smaller groups of five to twelve appear to be the norm. This number is close to the average crew of rock art ships, and they may therefore depict a crew of kóryos members. The more uncommon larger ships may depict several kóryos groups that have temporarily merged before a large-scale raid, much like other kóryos groups in different IE contexts. A boat depicted with a crew of 13 may consist of a kóryos group of twelve with a senior leader or instructor commanding the ship. This last motif may be especially pronounced in some panels.

#### 3.2.4. Senior Leaders and Instructors

Unlike warrior motifs, oarsmen in boats are predominately depicted as anonymous with little to no discernible features. In some boat motifs dated to the late NBA, enlarged warriors are depicted on the stern and bow of the ship among smaller oarsmen (Fig. 5). This motif may reflect social positions within the crew, as the larger figures appear to be commanding the boats (Ling 2008: 202ff, Ling & Cornell 2017: 20). However, more specifically, these larger figures may represent senior leaders and instructors of kóryos groups.

**[Figure 5. A panel in Tanum depicting a larger horned warrior commanding a boat crew. Author: Tanums Hällristningsmuseum (2008) ©shfa.dh.gu.se.]**

As mentioned in the previous chapter, kóryos members were typically initiated into the warband around ages ten to 15 and inhabited their new identity until age 20. In the European BA, Bergerbrant (2014: 524) argues that individuals seem to be regarded as adults, rather than children, around the age of 15 due to differences in grave goods. Because of this, I argue that 15 may have been the age elite males were initiated into kóryos groups during the NBA while 20 marked the transition into legal adulthood, constituting a 5-year-long kóryos period. However, some warriors in later Germanic and Celtic kóryos groups never lost their liminal identity despite reaching adulthood. They continue to live as kóryos members and may serve as either leaders or instructors of younger members. This is especially common in Germanic groups where senior warriors often became the masters of youth warbands (Enright 1996: 203). This is also reflected in the Norse saga *Bjarkamál*, where the older *berserkir* Bödvar becomes the mentor of Hjalti and guides him through his initiation (Harris 2008: 306). Rock art motifs containing an enlarged warrior among smaller boat crew figures may depict similar situations where a senior instructor commands a crew of kóryos, perhaps before a raid. The larger warrior figures, often depicted with bull horns, may further support this interpretation. Depictions of animal hybrids in rock art and metalwork from the NBA, especially bull-horned anthropomorphic figures, appear to be strongly connected to warriorhood and the elite (Ahlqvist & Vandkilde 2018). The horns of the larger figures may, in addition to their size, have acted as an identity marker for seniority in connection to kóryos groups.

Non-maritime depictions of differently-sized warrior figures could similarly represent kóryos instructors and members engaged in ritual, particularly weapon dances. For example, a panel in Tanum (Fig. 6) depicts two armed figures in a similar weapon dancing pose described earlier. The figure on the right is significantly larger than the one on the left. Similar to the boat motifs, the larger figure is horned, unlike the smaller non-horned figure. The panel at Kville with clear belt depictions mentioned earlier also contains enlarged horned warriors with a smaller non-horned warrior. Both panels may depict kóryos instructors and members engaged in weapon dances before a raid.

**[Figure 6. A panel in Tanum possibly depicting a kóryos member and a senior instructor engaged in weapon dancing. Author: Claes Claesson (1951) ©shfa.dh.gu.se.]**

The senior leaders and instructors of kóryos appear among Germanic and Celtic groups and they served an educational role for initiates in these contexts. It would seem reasonable that the adolescent members of kóryos needed to learn fighting techniques, raiding tactics, heroic poetry, and esoteric ritual knowledge from somewhere, most likely a type of instructor. NBA kóryos groups may have possessed similar instructor figures. These may have been older members who never left the group upon reaching adulthood, or former members now part of a secret society and tasked with educating future members through their kóryos period. The enlarged horned figures in rock art may thus depict senior instructors of NBA kóryos groups.

#### 3.2.5. Where Wolves?

The biggest issue with interpreting kóryos in NBA rock art is the lack of warrior figures with clear wolf elements, such as a wolf-head. Wolves and dogs do appear in some panels, but these are mostly hunting scenes (Gräslund 2014: 35). Several rock art panels depict figures with horns, which could indicate a helmet or a human-animal hybrid – a common motif in the NBA (Ahlqvist & Vandkilde 2018). However, no wolf-headed figures are depicted. While the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, the curious lack of wolf-warriors does raise theoretical questions. However, the lack of clear kóryos depictions may result from how rock art was made rather than the absence of kóryos groups.

Rock art depicts a narrow and idealized social reality in which only some aspects were considered important to depict. This limited choice seems to be motivated both ideologically to accentuate and reproduce certain social orders and magically to bring about a desired outcome. In this sense, some attributes may have been more important in ritual settings. This may be the case for kóryos and wolves in NBA rock art. However, there is no straightforward answer. It appears that NBA kóryos groups may not have placed a large emphasis on the wolfishness of their identity. Perhaps warriors and wolves were considered one and the same during this period, which would have made wolf-like attributes superfluous, or perhaps even taboo, to depict in rock art warrior motifs. Furthermore, it may not have been an important attribute to accentuate in rituals related to rock art carving. If rock art was a magical device intended to affect a desired outcome, and if it was carved in association with maritime raiding expeditions, then attributes related to ecstatic fury such as nudity and weapon dancing could have been more important to highlight and thus magically influence than their connection to wolves.

It is important to not overdetermine the lack of wolf-warrior motifs in rock art as proof that kóryos did not exist in the NBA. Rather, researchers should investigate other kóryos-associated attributes, such as the naked and belted motif or weapon dances, for correspondence. As I have shown, several other kóryos attributes may correspond to NBA rock art motifs.

## 4. Conclusion and Discussion

Kóryos has largely been unexplored in archaeological research, partially due to the subject's troubled history. However, recent aDNA studies have contributed to the growing field of archaeolinguistics, which has led to a renewed interest in IE linguistics for archaeologists. Despite this, only two studies have explored archaeological evidence of kóryos in the BA (Anthony & Brown 2017, Kaliff & Østigård 2022a). Barring one of these (Kaliff & Østigård 2022a), kóryos has only been briefly considered in NBA research, and no extensive study has interpreted kóryos in NBA rock art. In this thesis, I aimed to situate kóryos groups in the NBA. Specifically, I aimed to compare and identify similarities between kóryos attributes and rock art motifs from the NBA. Furthermore, a structural analysis of warriorship in the NBA was presented to avoid overdetermining rock art's role in my interpretations. The result of the comparative analysis was used to situate kóryos in the NBA and to explore the praxis, rituals, and ideology of NBA-specific kóryos groups. My research questions were: From an Indo-European comparative perspective, what are the most common attributes of kóryos groups? What are the similarities between these kóryos attributes and rock art motifs from the Nordic Bronze Age? What can these similarities inform about the praxis, rituals, and ideology of hypothesized kóryos groups in the NBA?

Using the theoretical framework of secret societies, formulated by Brian Hayden (2018), and applied to NBA contexts by Ling et al. (2018a, 2022), it is argued that an elite segment of society consisting of warriors, traders, ritual specialists, and boat-building craftsmen organized themselves into secret societies that controlled trade and warfare in the NBA. These groups consolidated political control by appropriating agro-pastoral surpluses to finance maritime trading and raiding expeditions to secure metals, slaves, and other commodities. Rock art is argued to have been magical devices carved by secret society members during rituals intended to ensure the success of maritime voyages and raids. Magic is understood as an active process intended to bring about a desired practical result during rituals. Rock art is also understood as an ideological construction, acting as a "model of" and a "model for" a specific social reality its carver wanted to reproduce. Ideology in this thesis is understood as social actions, ideas, and objects that consciously or unconsciously reproduce power structures.

In Chapter 2, I used a wide range of sources, such as textual accounts, myths, iconographic motifs, and linguistic studies, to construct a kóryos ideal type based on a set of attributes that frequently appear together in several IE contexts. This includes material from Indo-Iranian, Greek, Italic, Germanic, Celtic, Hittite, Balto-Slavic, and possible PIE contexts, covering a temporal range between 4000 BCE to 1300 CE. Specifically, the material was chosen based on the occurrence of attributes typically associated with kóryos in previous research, such as warbands, young males, elites, wolves, death, nudity, weapon dancing, and ecstatic fury. I synthesized these attributes in semi-sequential order, focusing on the praxis, rituals, and ideology of kóryos groups, from initiation to reintegration into society. I show that kóryos groups typically consisted of young males with elite backgrounds initiated around the age of ten to 15. Groups typically consisted of five to twelve members, but they may have temporarily merged during some periods forming parties of 50 to 150. During initiation, members swore an oath to the group and received a new liminal identity associated with wolves, dogs, lawlessness, and death, perhaps being considered dead themselves. They were also given a weapon, typically a spear, and a belt that may have symbolized loyalty to the warband. Members would enter a state of ecstatic fury prior to, or during, raids and battles. This was achieved through ritual and magical means and may have involved weapon dancing, chanting, psychoactive substances, and, especially in Germanic and Celtic contexts, nudity. Ecstatic fury was similarly connected to poetic inspiration, and members would memorize and recite heroic poetry as part of their duties. Their patron gods typically reflected the attributes of the warband. This included a connection to the elite, warriors, ecstatic fury, nudity, magic, and poetry. Around the age of 20, members would reintegrate into society as legal adults, often with high-status positions due to their elite backgrounds. However, some would continually live as kóryos members despite reaching adulthood. These senior members may have served as leaders or instructors for younger kóryos groups.

I argue that kóryos was originally a form of warrior education that fostered social cohesion among elite males and schooled them in fighting techniques, raiding tactics, and ritual matters. Identifying as wolves and entering states of ecstatic fury allowed members to auto-dehumanize themselves before raids, which promoted a willingness to kill and served as an ideological justification for it. Ultimately, kóryos should be seen as an ideological construction that acted to reproduce stratified, raiding-based socioeconomic structures in IE contexts. No kóryos group matches this ideal type exactly, and this is expected due to the vast spatial and temporal scale, as well as differing historical developments and trajectories. However, many of these attributes frequently appear together as a package. This indicates that a form of kóryos resembling the ideal type may have existed in PIE and subsequent post-PIE contexts. Specifically, this makes kóryos suitable for interpretation in the NBA.

In Chapter 3, I use the kóryos ideal type argued for in Chapter 2 to situate kóryos in the NBA. Before the rock art section, I present the Maritime Mode of Production (MMP) to situate warbands in NBA society and contextualize my interpretations. It is argued that warbands were institutionalized under elite control in this period, and they served to uphold the MMP by raiding and protecting trade routes. Warbands thus served an important role in continually reproducing the stratified, raiding-based socioeconomic structures of the NBA. A maritime-focused form of kóryos is argued to be reflected in the institutionalized warbands of the MMP during this period. Furthermore, similarities between Germanic and Celtic kóryos groups of later periods, such as a larger focus on nudity and spears compared to other IE contexts, are argued to have arisen as a result of long-distance trade ventures between Paleo-Germanic and Paleo-Celtic groups during the NBA. Since kóryos groups may have been part of these expeditions, similarities could have evolved as they mutually influenced each other during contact.

In the rock art section of Chapter 3, I argue that motifs containing armed and naked anthropomorphic figures with one or two raised arms depict weapon dancers. Similarly to later Iron Age weapon dancing motifs, the figures are depicted as naked with a slight bend in the knees and raised arms. Spears are commonly wielded and held in an active position overhead, but the spear tip is rarely depicted as touching or killing other figures. This is argued to depict an active dancing motion, rather than scenes of combat. Furthermore, the "adorant" pose is argued to similarly depict unarmed weapon dances due to the figures' similar poses in both motifs. I show that the "naked and belted" motif, found in several later Germanic and Celtic contexts, can be found in NBA rock art. Phallic figures are often depicted with weapons, such as spears or swords, which indicates a coupling of male nudity and warriorhood in NBA rock art. Unlike spears, swords are almost always depicted in a sheath hanging by the waist. Sheathed swords indirectly depict a belt and phallic figures with sheathed swords are thus argued to depict the naked and belted motif. One largely unexplored panel in Kville highlighted in this thesis clearly depicts belts around the waist of the figures. Several of these naked and belted figures are also depicted in the weapon dancing pose. The typical crew size of rock art boats in Sweden is around six to twelve or 13 individuals, but larger boats with crew sizes of 135 appear as well. This is argued to reflect the average size of kóryos groups, between five to twelve members, which may point to some correspondence. Furthermore, the large boats are argued to represent large kóryos groups that have temporarily merged. Horned warrior figures are depicted in commanding positions on boats and in weapon dancing motifs in rock art. They are typically larger than non-horned figures and are thus argued to be senior leaders or instructors of kóryos groups. They may be adult secret society members who take on the role of instructors for kóryos groups. Their duties may have been, in addition to participating in raids and rituals, to school the younger members in the skills they were expected to possess before becoming adults and entering elite groups themselves.

In my analysis, I have shown that several kóryos attributes correspond to commonly occurring rock art motifs. While wolves are noticeably absent in these depictions, the occurrence of several other attributes in rock art indicates that kóryos groups may have existed in the NBA. This period has been largely unexplored in previous kóryos research, barring one example. This thesis has expanded the purview of kóryos research into a textless period.

NBA kóryos groups were likely maritime-based warbands that raided for commodities, such as slaves, and protected long-distance trade expeditions. Furthermore, due to similarities between secret societies and kóryos groups, such as a focus on elites, warfare, raiding, magic, and animal symbolism, I argue that kóryos may have served as a "school" or initiation for future secret society members during the NBA. Members may have, in addition to raiding, learned necessary skills related to sea voyaging, trade, and ritual during a five-year-long kóryos period between the ages of 15 and 20. If rock art was a magical device intended to produce a desired outcome, these motifs may have been carved in connection to rituals intended to cause or enhance a state of ecstatic fury in NBA kóryos members before raids. Since many later kóryos attributes appear to be connected to ecstatic fury rituals, particularly nudity and weapon dancing, they most likely served similar purposes for NBA kóryos groups as well. In a practical sense, it seems unlikely that kóryos members were always naked, especially in the northern hemisphere. It is more likely that nudity was primarily present in ecstatic fury rituals and warfare, rather than a constant feature of life. The frequently reoccurring ithyphallic warrior motif indicates that it was a particularly important ritual feature, especially if rock art was carved during rituals connected to raids or sea voyages. Nudity may have been a visual identity marker for kóryos members, but importantly, a ritual method to accentuate, achieve, and magically enhance ecstatic fury. This state of fury had libidinal and masculine associations, as evidenced in the pronounced phalluses of warriors in both NBA rock and other IE contexts. Weapon dancing most likely had similar magico-ritual purposes, and weapon dancing motifs may have been carved in connection with actual weapon dances performed by kóryos members in ritualistic settings, paired with psychoactive substances and nudity to achieve ecstatic fury before raids. Rock art, as a source of social knowledge, also served to accentuate, promulgate, and co-constitute kóryos ideology to members and non-initiates by actively shaping the socioeconomic structures of the NBA, acting as a "model of" and "model for" a specific social reality. In this sense, rock art motifs were both ideological representations and magical devices intended to depict kóryos rituals and magically bring about their desired outcome. The long temporal scale in which these rituals survived in myth and rock art testifies to the success of kóryos ideology in Scandinavia and the rest of the Indo-European world.
